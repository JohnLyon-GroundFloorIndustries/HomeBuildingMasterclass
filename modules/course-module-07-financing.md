# Module 7 — Financing

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not financial advice; not yet reviewed by a lender.** Almost everything here is general practice [E] because lender rules are set bank by bank. Use the questions to get real answers. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will have committed funding that covers the baseline budget plus contingency, a draw schedule matched to your cash needs, and a payment process.

**Before you start:** baseline budget (Module 6), plans and specifications, permit status, land ownership or contract, two years of income documents.

---

### 7.1 Build sources and uses
- **What:** a one-page table. Uses: land, soft costs, hard costs, financing costs, contingency. Sources: cash, land equity, loan, other.
- **Standard:** sources equal uses, with at least 10% contingency inside uses. List sales tax, loan fees, and an interest reserve as uses. Your own labor is not a source of funds.
- **Done when:** the table balances without optimistic assumptions.

### 7.2 Choose the funding structure
- **Options [E]:**

| Option | Fit | Advantages | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Cash | Either track | No lender rules, no interest, full owner-builder freedom | Ties up capital; no outside discipline |
| B. Construction-to-permanent loan | T1 | One closing; converts to a mortgage | Many lenders exclude owner-builders or require a licensed builder of record |
| C. Construction-only loan, then refinance | T1 | More lenders to choose from | Two closings; rate risk at refinance |
| D. Lot equity as down payment | T1 | Owned land counts toward equity | Appraisal decides how much |
| E. Home equity line on another property | Either | Flexible; few construction rules | Puts that property at risk; variable rate |
| F. Commercial or private construction loan | T2 | Built for spec projects | Higher rate and fees; lower leverage; short term |
| G. VA construction loan | T1, eligible veterans | Low or no down payment | Few lenders offer it. VA ended its builder identification number requirement in March 2025, and its appraisal guidance has provided for a veteran acting as general contractor on a home for their own occupancy [V]. The obstacle is the lender: most require a licensed, insured third-party builder [E]. Ask directly. |

- **Recommendation:** decide by asking lenders first (7.3). The structure that exists for owner-builders in your market is the one you can choose.
- **Track:** owner-occupied loan products require you to occupy. Using one for a spec build is misrepresentation.

### 7.3 Shortlist lenders
- **What:** two or three lenders who confirm in writing that they will lend on your structure.
- **How:** call local banks and credit unions first; they are likelier to lend to owner-builders than national lenders [E]. Use the script below.
- **Done when:** you have written terms from at least two.

### 7.4 Assemble the loan package
- **What:** plans, specifications with finish allowances, baseline budget in the lender's format, schedule, permit or permit status, land documents, your experience summary, and financials.
- **Standard:** lenders judge owner-builders on experience and reserves. Write an honest experience page: projects, skills, and which scopes are hired to licensed trades.
- **Do now for later:** the budget format here becomes the draw schedule.

### 7.5 Apply and complete the appraisal (wait)
- **What:** loan approval and an appraisal of the finished house from plans.
- **Standard:** the loan is sized on the lower of cost or appraised value [E]. Under Fannie Mae's single-closing rules, if you do not own the lot when the loan first funds, the loan-to-value ratio uses the lesser of lot price plus construction cost or the as-completed appraisal; if you already own the lot, it uses the as-completed appraisal [V]. Cost means cash cost: your labor is not in it and is not counted as your down payment [V]. Lenders that keep their loans set their own rules; ask each which test applies and whether cost includes sales tax, loan fees, an interest reserve, and contingency [E]. Give the appraiser recent comparable sales and your specification sheet.
- **If it goes wrong:** the appraisal is below cost. You bring more cash, cut cost (6.9), or challenge with better comparables.

### 7.6 Negotiate the draw schedule
- **What:** a written schedule of payments tied to completed stages.
- **How:** align draws with your real cash needs: materials are bought before a stage is complete. Ask for a draw at delivery of large packages (lumber, trusses, windows) or a materials deposit provision.
- **Standard:** lenders pay for work in place, verified by their inspector [E]. Ask how many days from request to funds, what each inspection costs, and whether lien waivers are required with each draw.
- **If it goes wrong:** you run out of cash between stages. Keep a working reserve equal to the largest single stage [E].

### 7.7 Close the loan (wait)
- **What:** signed loan documents and recorded deed of trust.
- **Standard:** a construction lien has priority over a deed of trust that was unrecorded when that claimant began labor or professional services or first delivered materials or equipment [V]. That is why many lenders and their title insurers require that no work or deliveries occur before the loan records [E]. Tell the lender about design, survey, septic, and clearing work already done, and ask what they need from those firms [E]. Ask before you move any dirt or accept any delivery.
- **Point of no return:** closing.

### 7.8 Set up the project account and payment process
- **What:** one account for all project money, and a rule for approving payments.
- **How:** every payment needs: a matching budget code, an invoice or receipt, and for trades, inspection of the work and a lien waiver (10.10).
- **Standard:** two signatures in the household on any payment above a set amount.

### 7.9 Run each draw
- **What:** a repeatable draw routine.
- **How:** 1) Update the cost report. 2) Request the draw with photos and invoices. 3) Meet the lender's inspector on site. 4) Provide lien waivers. 5) Reconcile funds received against the budget.
- **Crew, time:** 1 person, 2–3 hours per draw.
- **If it goes wrong:** the inspector certifies less complete than you claimed. Ask what is missing, finish it, and re-request.

### 7.10 Convert or pay off at completion
- **What:** T1: conversion to permanent financing after the certificate of occupancy. T2: payoff at sale closing.
- **Standard:** know the loan's maturity date and extension cost from day one. Construction loans commonly run 9–12 months with paid extensions [E]; an owner-pace build may need longer. A single-closing loan sold to Fannie Mae may have no construction period longer than 12 months and no more than 18 months in total [V]; a lender that keeps the loan can allow more. Negotiate the term in 7.6, not at month eleven.

- [ ] Payment process written

## Top mistakes
1. Assuming a lender will finance an owner-builder (7.3).
2. A draw schedule that pays after you have already had to buy (7.6).
3. A loan term shorter than the real schedule (7.10).
4. Starting work before the loan records (7.7).

## Call script — lender
"We are building our own home as owner-builders: we act as general contractor and do much of the work, with licensed electricians and plumbers where required. Do you offer construction financing for that? What equity, credit, reserves, and experience do you require? How do you treat our own labor in the budget? What term can we get, and what do extensions cost? How do draws work and how long do they take?"
