# Module 1 — Go/No-Go and Choosing a Track

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not yet reviewed by a professional.** [V] verified, [E] estimate. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will have a one-page decision memo: build or don't, which track, the budget ceiling, and the hours you are committing.

**Before you start:** 12 months of household finances, a list of your tools and equipment, your work and family calendar for the next two years.

**Why it comes first:** every later module spends money. This one costs a weekend and three phone calls.

---

### 1.1 Define the finished result
- **What:** one paragraph stating what you are building, where, for whom, and by when.
- **How:** write size range, bedrooms and baths, area, target move-in or sale date, and the one thing that would make the project a failure.
- **Standard:** specific enough that a stranger could tell whether you hit it.
- **Done when:** both adults in the household have signed the paragraph.
- **Output:** project statement, first page of the binder.

### 1.2 Audit skills and equipment
- **What:** a table of every trade scope scored for your ability and equipment.
- **How:** 1) List the scopes from the DIY matrix in the course outline. 2) Score each: done it at this scale, done it small, never done it. 3) Mark the equipment you own, can rent, or lack. 4) Mark who in the family does it and who is the second pair of hands.
- **Standard:** "done it small" counts as never for foundations, roofing, and anything inspected for structure.
- **If it goes wrong:** the audit is flattering. Have someone who has worked beside you score it too.
- **Output:** skills and equipment audit.

### 1.3 Budget your time
- **What:** hours per week you can give, for how many months, with known gaps.
- **How:** 1) Count realistic weekly hours after work, family, and rest. 2) Block out known absences. 3) Multiply by months. 4) Compare to a rough labor need of 2,500–4,000 owner hours for a house this size [E]. The low end holds only if concrete finishing, roofing, drywall finishing, and most mechanical work are hired; one reviewer judged even that optimistic, so plan on the upper half.
- **Standard:** two people at 20 hours a week each give about 2,000 hours a year. If the math needs more than two years, plan to hire more.
- **If it goes wrong:** the schedule depends on a single person. Name the scopes you will hire if that person is unavailable for 60 days.
- **Output:** time budget.

### 1.4 Set the budget ceiling
- **What:** the most you will spend, and the cash you can put in.
- **How:** 1) Total liquid cash you will commit. 2) Estimate borrowing capacity (confirmed in Module 7). 3) Subtract a household reserve of at least six months of expenses [E]. 4) The remainder is the ceiling for land, soft costs, construction, and contingency together.
- **Standard:** the ceiling does not move later because the plans grew.
- **Output:** budget ceiling, signed.

### 1.5 Price your own labor
- **What:** an honest estimate of what your labor saves.
- **How:** 1) For each scope you will do, find a rough hired price (two phone quotes or a local builder's ranges). 2) Subtract materials. 3) The difference is labor value. 4) Divide by your estimated hours for an hourly figure.
- **Standard:** savings are commonly 15–30% of construction cost for families who self-perform most non-licensed work [E]. As a cross-check, builders in a 2024 national survey reported overhead of 5.7% and profit of 11.0% of sale price against construction cost of 64.4%, so the general contractor's share alone is about a quarter of construction cost on a house built for sale [V]. Treat anything above 30% as a warning. Labor savings on your own home are not taxed, so compare your hourly figure with your take-home pay, not your gross pay [E].
- **If it goes wrong:** the hourly figure is below what you earn at work. That is information, not failure; some scopes are better hired.
- **Output:** labor value table.

### 1.6 Test the owner-occupied numbers [T1]
- **What:** a comparison of building against buying an equivalent house.
- **How:** total cost to build (land, site, fees, construction, financing, contingency) against the price of a comparable existing or new home nearby.
- **Standard:** include what you will pay to live elsewhere during the build; sales tax on every material and on the full price of every hired contractor, 9.2% in unincorporated Kitsap County in late 2026 [V] (many bids are quoted before tax); loan fees and interest; and property tax during the build. Compare against the full cost of buying, including a buyer's closing costs [E].
- **Done when:** you can state the dollar advantage or disadvantage in one sentence.
- **Output:** build-versus-buy sheet.

### 1.7 Test the spec numbers [T2]
- **What:** a one-page pro forma showing profit after every cost.
- **How:** expected sale price from three recent comparable sales, minus land, site, fees, construction, financing interest, insurance, sales costs, excise tax, and income tax. Then test two bad cases: price 10% lower, and sale six months later.
- **Standard:** the state excise tax on a sale is 1.1% of the price up to $525,000 and 1.28% of the part above that up to $1,525,000 through 2026; from 1 Jan 2027 those thresholds are $551,000 and $1,551,000 [V]. Kitsap County adds 0.5% [V]. On a $700,000 sale in 2027 the total is about $11,470. Agent commissions and closing costs are in addition [E]. Construction cost must include sales tax on materials and on hired contractors' full charges; a spec builder cannot recover it [V]. For income tax, plan on the profit being ordinary income and, if the build is a business, subject to 15.3% self-employment tax as well [V]. Use the CPA's combined rate, not a guess.
- **Track:** T2 work on a house built for sale is limited by contractor and trade licensing law. Do not finalize this sheet until Module 2.
- **If it goes wrong:** profit disappears in either bad case. The margin is too thin for a first project.
- **Output:** spec pro forma with two stress cases.

### 1.8 Run three reality-check conversations (wait)
- **What:** notes from a lender, a builder, and a real estate agent.
- **How:** book 30–60 minutes each. Bring the project statement and your numbers.
- **Who and what to ask:**
    - Lender: "Do you lend to owner-builders acting as their own general contractor? What equity, reserves, and experience do you require?"
    - Builder: "What do first-time owner-builders get wrong here? What would this house cost you to build?"
    - Agent: "What sells fastest in this area and at what price? What do buyers reject?"
- **Money out:** offer to pay the builder for the hour.
- **Done when:** each conversation has changed or confirmed at least one number.
- **Output:** three sets of notes.

### 1.9 Decide and write the memo
- **What:** a one-page go/no-go memo.
- **How:** state the decision, track, ceiling, hours, top three risks, and the conditions under which you will stop.
- **Options:** go on T1; go on T2; go on T1 now and T2 later; no-go; delay 6–12 months to build cash or skills.
- **Recommendation:** T1 first for most families. It allows the most owner labor under Washington law and has the most forgiving financing.
- **Done when:** both adults sign.
- **Output:** decision memo.

## Module gate
- [ ] Project statement signed

- [ ] Decision memo signed, including stop conditions

## Top mistakes
1. Valuing owner labor at zero cost and unlimited supply (1.3, 1.5).
2. Using cost per square foot as the budget (1.6).
3. Choosing T2 before learning what the law allows (1.7).
