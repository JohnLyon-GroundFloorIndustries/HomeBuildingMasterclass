# Module 2 — Legal Structure, Licensing, and Tax

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not legal or tax advice; not yet reviewed by an attorney or CPA.** [V] verified, [E] estimate. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will have a signed matrix of who may legally perform each scope on your project, an ownership structure, and a tax plan for your track.

**Before you start:** the decision memo from Module 1.

**Why it matters:** in Washington the two tracks are legally different projects. Getting this wrong can void permits, trigger penalties, and expose you on sworn affidavits.

---

### 2.1 Map who may legally do each scope
- **What:** a matrix of every scope with three columns: owner may do it, registered contractor required, licensed trade required.
- **How:** fill it from the rules below, then mark each [V] item with the statute to show the attorney.
- **Rules:**
    - General construction: a person working on their own property is exempt from contractor registration, but not when the work is done for the purpose of selling or leasing the property. That purpose test has no time limit [V]. Separately, anyone who offers a property for sale without having occupied or used the structure for more than one year after substantial completion is defined as a contractor, unless they contracted with a registered general contractor and did not superintend the work [V]. The limit for property owned under twelve months is a different rule: it applies to an owner who hires a registered contractor to run the project [V].
    - Electrical: an owner may wire their own property unless it is a new building intended for rent, sale, or lease, or is offered for sale within twelve months of obtaining it [V]. The state's owner affidavit for a new single-family home is made under penalty of perjury and says three things: the house is not intended for rent, sale, or lease; you will live there at least 24 months after completing the electrical work; and you will do the electrical work personally [V]. The statute separately says a householder may receive help from a friend, neighbor, or relative who is not in the electrical trade [V]. Ask the state electrical inspector how that fits with "personally" before anyone else pulls wire.
    - Plumbing: no plumber certificate is required to do plumbing at your own residence or on other property you own, and a householder may receive help from a friend, neighbor, or relative who is not in the plumbing trade [V]. The plumbing contractor license rule exempts a person contracting for plumbing work on their own residence, but not on a building that is for rent, sale, or lease [V]. The text does not settle whether an owner may personally plumb a house built for sale. Treat T2 plumbing as hired work unless the department or your attorney says otherwise in writing [E].
    - Low-voltage: an owner may do telecommunications work on their own property without a telecommunications contractor license, except on a new building intended for rent, sale, or lease, or on property offered for sale within twelve months of obtaining it [V].
- **Output:** legal scope matrix, draft.

### 2.2 Decide your occupancy commitment
- **What:** a written statement of how long you will live in the house.
- **How:** read three rules against your real plans, including a possible job move: the electrical affidavit (live there 24 months after the electrical work); the contractor definition (offering the house for sale before you have occupied it more than one year after substantial completion); and the purpose test (work done in order to sell is not exempt, however long you hold) [V]. Write down now why you are building. If you later have to sell early, what you intended when you did the work is the fact that matters, and it depends on evidence [E].
- **Standard:** the electrical affidavit is a sworn statement. Sign it only if the 24 months is realistic.
- **Track:** T1 only. T2 does not sign owner-occupancy affidavits.
- **If it goes wrong:** you may need to sell early. Ask the attorney in 2.3 what that exposes you to before you sign anything.
- **Output:** occupancy statement.

### 2.3 Meet a construction attorney (wait)
- **What:** written answers to your licensing, liability, and contract questions.
- **How:** send the matrix and occupancy statement ahead. Ask:
    1. "Is this matrix right for our facts?"
    2. "If we build as owner-occupants and must sell early, what are the consequences?"
    3. "For a spec build, what is the cleanest lawful structure?"
    4. "What warranty and disclosure obligations will we have when we sell?"
    5. "Will you review our subcontract template?"
- **Money out:** one to three hours of attorney time [E].
- **Done when:** you have answers in writing.
- **Output:** attorney memo or email.

### 2.4 Choose the contractor path [T2]
- **What:** a decision on how the spec build is lawfully performed.
- **Options:**

| Option | What it means | Cost and effort | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Register as a general contractor | You build under your own registration; licensed trades still do electrical and plumbing | Bond, insurance, fee; days to weeks | You carry contractor liability |
| B. Hire a registered general contractor | They run the job; your labor is limited | Their fee, often 10–20% [E] | Lowest legal risk; lowest sweat equity |
| C. Build it as your home, live in it, sell later | T1 rules apply | Time | Not a spec path. If the purpose of the build is to sell, the owner exemption does not apply however long you hold [V] |
| D. Do not do a spec build yet | — | — | — |

- **Recommendation:** A for a family that intends to build more than once; C only if the house is truly being built as your home. Confirm with the attorney.
- **If it goes wrong:** working as a contractor without registration is a gross misdemeanor and also an infraction with a penalty of $1,200 to $10,000, and each day and each worksite is a separate infraction [V]. A building permit obtained by falsifying information to claim an exemption is forfeited [V]. An unregistered contractor cannot sue to collect for the work [V].
- **Output:** decision recorded in the matrix.

### 2.5 Register as a contractor [T2, if chosen] (wait)
- **What:** an active registration with the Department of Labor & Industries.
- **How:** 1) Register the business with the Department of Revenue. 2) Buy a continuous surety bond. 3) Buy general liability insurance naming the department as certificate holder. 4) Submit the notarized application and fee [V].
- **Standard:** general contractor bond $30,000 since 1 July 2024; liability insurance of $200,000 public liability and $50,000 property damage, or $250,000 combined [V]. No exam [V].
- **Who:** insurance agent for bond and policy; local department office can notarize.
- **Done when:** your registration shows active on the department's Verify tool.
- **If it goes wrong:** a bond or policy lapse suspends the registration. Calendar renewals.

### 2.6 Choose the ownership structure
- **What:** a decision on who holds title: yourselves, or an entity.
- **Options:** individual ownership (simplest; required in practice for most owner-occupied loans [E]; the state's electrical affidavit for a new single-family home is a personal statement that you will live there and do the work yourself, which a company cannot make [V]); a limited liability company (common for spec; affects financing and insurance). If a company holds title, the company is the owner. For a spec build the company is then the party that registers as the contractor, and whether its members may personally use any owner exemption depends on facts the attorney must review [E].
- **Standard (state):** to be taxed as a speculative builder, the same legal person must own the land and do the building. The Department of Revenue looks past title to who intended to own, who paid for the land, who paid for the improvements, and how lenders dealt with it [V]. A company that builds on land owned by its members, officers, or partners is taxed as a contractor making a retail sale, not as a speculative builder: it owes retailing business tax and must collect sales tax on the construction, labor included [V]. Expect the same result if you own the land personally and build through your own separately registered company [E]. Put the land, the contractor registration, the material purchases, and the labor in one name.
- **Standard (federal):** an LLC with one member is ignored for income tax unless it elects otherwise, so the house is reported on the owner's own return; an LLC with two or more members is a partnership and files its own return [V]. Either can elect corporate or S corporation treatment. The entity does not change whether spec profit is ordinary income. It can change self-employment tax, payroll filings, and whether the home-sale exclusion is available if you later move in. Ask the CPA how a married couple's LLC is classified in a community property state, and whether an S corporation is worth its cost for one house [E].
- **Who:** attorney and CPA together.
- **Done when:** the structure is chosen before any purchase offer.
- **If it goes wrong:** title vests in the wrong name. Fixing it later costs fees and may trigger tax.

### 2.7 Meet a CPA (wait)
- **What:** a written tax plan for your track.
- **How:** ask:
    1. "T1: what records do I need to establish my cost basis, and what are the rules for excluding gain when I eventually sell my home?"
    2. "T2: on our facts (first house, our other income, how we hold title), is the profit ordinary income from a business, and is it subject to self-employment tax?"
    3. "T2: confirm speculative builder treatment for state sales and business taxes, and tell us whether we must register with the Department of Revenue."
    4. "Which costs are added to the cost of the house, may we deduct or must we capitalize construction interest and property tax, and how should we track them?"
    5. "T2: what estimated tax payments are due, and when, in the year of sale?"
    6. "If we rent the house or move in instead of selling, or must sell a house we built to live in sooner than planned, what changes?"
- **Standard (state, T2):** a speculative builder pays sales tax on all materials, tools, and supplies, and on the full charge of every contractor it hires, labor included; it may not use a reseller permit for them [V]. It does not owe business tax or collect sales tax on the value of its own construction, and the sale of the finished real estate is exempt from business tax and is not a retail sale [V]. Anything bought without sales tax, such as some online or out-of-state orders, owes use tax at the job-site rate [V]. If the buyer gets title or an immediate right to possession while the house is under construction, work after that date is a retail sale: you owe retailing business tax and collect sales tax on that part of the price [V]. A purchase agreement that closes after completion does not by itself do this [V].
- **Standard (state, T1):** an owner building their own home is a consumer. You pay sales tax on materials, tools, and rentals, and every contractor you hire must charge sales tax on its full price, labor and materials [V]. Use tax applies to untaxed purchases [V]. Building or later selling your own home creates no business tax and no registration duty [E]. The combined rate in unincorporated Kitsap County is 9.2% in late 2026; check the rate for the delivery address [V].
- **Standard (state registration, T2):** you must register with the Department of Revenue if you have $12,000 or more a year of gross income taxable under the business tax, must collect or pay any tax to the department, or need any other state business license [V]. Real estate sale proceeds are exempt, so for most spec builders the trigger is the contractor registration (2.5), forming an LLC, or a sale during construction [E]. Ask the CPA to confirm for your structure.
- **Standard (federal, T1):** your basis is what the house cost you in money: land, materials, paid labor, permits, design, utility connections, and the sales tax paid on them. The value of your own labor, and of unpaid help, is not part of basis [V]. When you sell a main home you can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned it and lived in it for two of the five years before the sale [V]. The two years of use start when you move in, not when you buy the land [E]. A sale sooner than that because of a job move of 50 miles or more, a health reason, or an unforeseeable event can qualify for a prorated exclusion [V]. A service member on qualified extended duty can elect to suspend the five-year period for up to ten years, but two years of actual use are still required [V]. Interest on a construction loan can be treated as home mortgage interest for up to 24 months if the house becomes your home when it is ready [V]. Building, living in briefly, and selling repeatedly can make you a dealer; ask the CPA before the second house [E].
- **Standard (federal, T2):** a house built to sell is property held for sale to customers, not a capital asset, so profit is ordinary income whatever the holding period [V]. If the activity is a trade or business the profit is also subject to self-employment tax of 15.3%, with the 12.4% Social Security part ending at an annual earnings cap [V]. Whether one house is a trade or business depends on your facts. Costs of building are added to the cost of the house and recovered at sale, not deducted as you spend [V]. Most small builders are exempt from the uniform capitalization rules, and a taxpayer may elect to capitalize taxes and carrying charges [V]; which treatment of construction interest and property tax applies to you is a CPA question. Your own labor is not a cost and is never deducted; it shows up as profit [V]. A sale puts the whole tax bill in one year: estimated payments are generally required if you will owe $1,000 or more [V]. If you pay an unincorporated trade $2,000 or more in a year (for payments from 2026) in the course of the business, collect a Form W-9 before paying and file Form 1099-NEC [V]. Homeowners paying for work on their own home do not file these [V].
- **Standard (federal, credits):** the homeowner credits for efficiency improvements and for solar or geothermal ended for property placed in service after 31 December 2025, and the builder credit for efficient new homes applies only to homes acquired through 30 June 2026 [V]. Do not put any of them in a budget. State and utility rebates are separate; check them directly.
- **Output:** CPA memo; chart of accounts.

### 2.8 Set up tax accounts and bookkeeping
- **What:** a separate bank account, a bookkeeping file using the cost codes from Module 6, and state registrations if required.
- **How:** open the account; route every project dollar through it; keep every receipt and delivery ticket.
- **Standard:** no personal spending in the project account.
- **Records:** keep the invoice for every payment, with sales tax shown as a separate amount; the state requires contractors to state it separately, and it is your proof the tax was paid [V]. Log any purchase made without sales tax and pay the use tax on it [V]. T2: collect a Form W-9 from each trade before the first payment, and set money aside for estimated income tax from the day you list [E]. Log your own hours for Module 21; they have no tax value but they are the measure of the project.
- **Registrations:** T1 individuals need none for tax [E]. T2: a state business license with tax registration comes with contractor registration or an LLC (see 2.7). Get a federal employer identification number if you form an entity or hire anyone [E].
- **Output:** account and bookkeeping file.

### 2.9 Plan for helpers
- **What:** a rule for anyone who works on the site who is not a registered contractor.
- **How:** separate three groups: unpaid family and friends, paid helpers, registered contractors. Ask the department and your insurance broker how each is covered.
- **Standard:** the department's guidance is that an owner who directly hires workers for new construction must provide workers' compensation insurance. The statute's exclusion for work at a private home covers only gardening, maintenance, and repair [V]. Whether a given paid helper is your worker depends on the facts: who directs the work, and whether the helper runs an independent, registered business [E]. Unpaid family and friends are generally outside workers' compensation, so an injury to one of them falls on your liability insurance or on you [E]. On T2, a registered contractor may not subcontract to or use an unregistered contractor, so paid help must be your employee or a registered contractor [V].
- **If it goes wrong:** a paid helper is injured without coverage. This is the largest uninsured risk on an owner-built job.
- **Output:** helper policy, one paragraph.

### 2.10 File the legal matrix
- **What:** the final matrix, signed by both of you, with the attorney's and CPA's answers attached.
- **Done when:** every scope has one lawful performer named.

- Largest correction in that round: the first draft misread the owner exemption as a twelve-month holding rule. Work done in order to sell is never exempt, whatever the holding period.

## Confirm with a professional
- Whether an owner may personally plumb a house built for sale, and whether helpers may assist on owner electrical work.
- Whether an owner may self-perform any plumbing or electrical on a spec house through employees of a registered contractor entity.
- Warranty and disclosure duties on sale of new construction.
- Federal tax on your facts: whether a single spec house is a trade or business, self-employment tax, treatment of construction interest and property tax, entity classification, and the home-sale exclusion if plans change.
- State tax: whether you must register with the Department of Revenue, and the result of any sale before completion.
