# Module 3 — Land

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not yet reviewed by a professional.** [V] verified, [E] estimate. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will own a lot that you have proven is buildable, with a known cost to make it ready, or you will have walked away with your deposit.

**Before you start:** decision memo (Module 1), ownership structure (Module 2), budget ceiling.

**Rule of the module:** you do not close until water, sewage, access, and buildable area are confirmed in writing.

---

### 3.1 Write lot criteria
- **What:** a one-page list of must-haves and deal-breakers.
- **How:** price cap, area, minimum size, utilities, slope, drive time, schools, and how much site cost you can absorb.
- **Track:** T2 criteria come from buyers, not from you: commute, schools, resale comparables.
- **Output:** lot criteria sheet.

### 3.2 Screen lots online
- **What:** a shortlist of three to five lots.
- **How:** for each listing, pull the county parcel details, zoning map, and critical areas layers. Note wetlands, streams, steep slopes, shoreline, flood zone, and nearby sewer or water lines.
- **Standard:** reject from the desk any lot whose mapped buffers leave no clear building area.
- **Crew, time:** 1 person, 30–60 minutes per lot.
- **Output:** screening sheet per lot.

### 3.3 Confirm jurisdiction and zoning
- **What:** written setbacks, height limit, lot coverage, and permitted use for the lot.
- **How:** identify whether the lot is in a city or unincorporated county. Read the zone's standards. Call or email planning staff to confirm.
- **Standard:** cities inside the county run their own permit process and may differ from the county [V].
- **Done when:** you can draw the legal building envelope on the plat.

### 3.4 Confirm legal lot and access
- **What:** proof the parcel is a legal building lot with legal, physical, and fire access.
- **How:** 1) Ask the county whether a legal lot determination is needed. 2) Read the title report for access easements. 3) Measure the access road.
- **Standard:** the county's buildable-lot guidance starts with verifying legal lot status [V]. State law bars building and septic permits for land divided in violation of the subdivision statute, with an exception for an innocent purchaser without actual notice and a right to damages or rescission against the seller [V]. Those remedies mean a lawsuit, so settle lot status before closing. Fire access roads serving single-family homes must be at least 20 ft wide with an approved turnaround; hydrants delivering at least 500 gallons per minute are usually sufficient [V].
- **If it goes wrong:** access is by handshake across a neighbor's land. Require, before closing, a recorded easement that runs with the land, covers utilities as well as travel, states its width and who maintains it, and that the title company agrees to insure as part of your policy [E].

### 3.5 Walk the lot
- **What:** photos and notes from a full walk of the boundaries and building area.
- **How:** bring the plat, a tape, a level app, and boots. Look for standing water, wetland plants, springs, slopes, old fill, dumped debris, neighbor encroachments, and where trucks would enter.
- **Standard:** walk it once in wet weather before closing.
- **Crew, time:** 2 people, 1–2 hours.

### 3.6 Check water supply
- **What:** written confirmation of how the house gets water.
- **Options:** A. Public water: ask the purveyor for availability, connection fee, and meter lead time. B. Private well: ask the health district about well siting and review nearby well logs for depth and yield.
- **Standard:** about one in five homes in the county lacks access to public water and works with the health district on a well [V].
- **Money out [E]:** connection fees or well drilling are each commonly five figures. Get the number, not a guess.
- **Done when:** you hold a letter or email from the purveyor, or a driller's estimate.

### 3.7 Check sewer or septic feasibility
- **What:** confirmation of sewage disposal: sewer available, or soil that supports a septic system for your bedroom count.
- **How (septic):** 1) Hire a licensed septic designer. 2) Designer evaluates soils and proposes system type, size, and location, with a reserve area. 3) Ask for the installed cost range for that system type.
- **Standard:** the health district requires a licensed septic designer to submit a Building Site Application proposing size, type, and location [V]. Bedroom count drives system size [E].
- **Do now for later:** the drainfield and reserve area must stay undisturbed through construction (11.7).
- **If it goes wrong:** soils need an engineered system at two to three times the cost [E], or no system fits. That is a walk-away finding.

### 3.8 Check power and telecom
- **What:** a written estimate from the power utility for service to the building site, and confirmation of internet options.
- **How:** request a new-service estimate with the site plan sketch; ask about transformer, trench length, and lead time.
- **Do now for later:** utility lead times can run months [E]. The date you apply drives 9.6.

### 3.9 Check critical areas, slopes, and flood
- **What:** a determination of what studies the county will require.
- **How:** take the mapped layers and your walk notes to planning staff. Ask which reports are needed: wetland delineation, geotechnical report, habitat assessment, shoreline review.
- **Standard:** the county reviews building placement near critical areas and their buffers through the building permit [V].
- **Options:** order reports before closing (costs money, removes doubt) or negotiate a longer feasibility period.
- **Recommendation:** order them when any mapped feature touches the building area.

### 3.10 Estimate site development cost
- **What:** one page totalling the cost to make the lot ready to build.
- **How:** add driveway, clearing, grading, erosion control, septic, water, power, stormwater measures, permits and impact fees, and studies.
- **Standard:** the county's 2026 example shows about $15,500 in permit and impact fees for a 2,000 sq ft, 3-bedroom house [V]. A new driveway needs a road approach permit [V].
- **Done when:** land price plus this total fits inside the land-and-site share of your ceiling.

### 3.11 Make an offer with a feasibility contingency
- **What:** a signed offer giving you time to finish due diligence and an exit if it fails.
- **How:** with your agent or attorney, include a feasibility period long enough for septic approval and any studies, and state that feasibility is at your sole discretion. The contract should also say how and by when you must give notice, and that the earnest money is refunded if you do. Read whether silence at the deadline ends the deal or waives the contingency; forms differ [E].
- **Standard:** 60–120 days is common for raw land; septic review alone can take a month or more [E].
- **If it goes wrong:** the seller refuses a real feasibility period. Pay for the septic design before offering, or walk.

### 3.12 Order title and survey (wait)
- **What:** a title commitment you have read, and marked property corners.
- **How:** read every exception: easements, covenants, road maintenance agreements. Hire a surveyor to set or confirm corners, and to map topography if the design needs it.
- **Standard:** covenants can restrict size, materials, and outbuildings more than zoning does.

### 3.13 Complete feasibility studies (wait)
- **What:** the septic application submitted or approved, and any required reports in hand.
- **How:** designer submits the Building Site Application; a health district inspector visits the site, listed as within 30 business days [V].
- **Standard:** you want approval, or a written designer opinion with soil logs, before the contingency expires.

### 3.14 Close or walk
- **What:** a decision made against a checklist.
- **How:** confirm each item: legal lot, access, water, sewage, power estimate, critical areas resolved, title clean, site cost within budget.
- **Done when:** every line is yes and you close, or one is no and you give written notice to terminate inside the feasibility period.
- **Point of no return:** closing.

## Module gate
- [ ] Water and sewage confirmed in writing
- [ ] Legal access and fire access confirmed
- [ ] Building envelope drawn with all setbacks and buffers
- [ ] Site development cost within budget
- [ ] Title exceptions read and accepted

## Top mistakes
1. Closing before septic and water are proven (3.6, 3.7, 3.14).
2. Underestimating driveway, power, and septic cost (3.10).
3. Missing a buffer that removes the building area (3.9).

## Call scripts
- **Planning staff:** "I'm considering parcel [number] for one single-family home. What zone is it, what are the setbacks, and which critical area reports would you expect?"
- **Septic designer:** "Can you evaluate this lot for a [3]-bedroom home before my feasibility period ends on [date]? What system type do you expect and what does it cost installed?"
- **Power utility:** "What will it cost and how long will it take to bring service to a new home at this location?"
