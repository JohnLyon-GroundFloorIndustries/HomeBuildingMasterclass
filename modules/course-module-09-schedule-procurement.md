# Module 9 — Schedule and Procurement

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not yet reviewed by a professional.** Durations are estimates [E]. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will have a baseline schedule at your real pace with every inspection and wait built in, and a procurement log that tells you what to order and when.

**Before you start:** step map, time budget (1.3), inspection list (5.12), budget (Module 6), hired scopes list (10.1).

---

### 9.1 List activities from the step map
- **What:** a list of schedule activities, one per build step or small group of steps from Modules 11–19.
- **How:** copy the step titles; merge steps that happen the same day; keep every inspection and every (wait) as its own line.
- **Options:** spreadsheet (enough for most), free scheduling software, or sticky notes on a wall. Choose the one you will update weekly.

### 9.2 Set durations at owner pace
- **What:** a working-hours estimate and a calendar duration for each activity.
- **How:** 1) Estimate crew-hours for the task. 2) Divide by the hours your crew gives per week (1.3). 3) Add a learning factor.
- **Standard:** a first-time crew commonly takes two to three times a professional crew's hours [E]. Use published professional durations only as the floor.
- **Reference ranges at owner pace, part-time, for two adults giving about 40 crew-hours a week in total [E]:** site work and foundation 8–14 weeks; framing to dry-in 14–26 weeks; envelope 6–10 weeks; rough-ins 8–12 weeks; insulation and drywall 5–9 weeks; finishes 16–28 weeks. Together these come to about 2,300–4,000 crew-hours, in line with the hour range in 1.3; the low ends assume concrete finishing, roofing, and drywall finishing are hired. Framing is the phase to shorten with helpers or a hired crew: a professional crew spends roughly 450–600 hours framing and drying in this house, and a first-time crew two to three times that, which will not fit one dry season at 40 hours a week [E].

### 9.3 Link dependencies and inspection holds
- **What:** each activity tied to what must finish first.
- **How:** use the step map's order and the dependency table in the course brief. Insert each inspection as a hold with two to five working days for scheduling [E].
- **Standard:** nothing that covers work is scheduled before the inspection that approves it.

### 9.4 Find the critical path and weather windows
- **What:** the chain of activities that sets the finish date, and the calendar dates of weather-sensitive work.
- **How:** 1) Trace the longest chain from start to finish; delay on it delays everything. 2) Place earthwork, foundation, framing, and roofing against the local dry season.
- **Standard:** in western Washington, aim to be dried in before the fall rains [E]. If the schedule shows framing in November, either start earlier, hire the framing, or wait for spring.
- **Decision:** if framing to dry-in cannot fit May through September at your real hours, hire a framing crew for the shell only: floor, walls, trusses, and roof sheathing. It is the most effective schedule and safety decision an owner-builder makes [E].
- **Options to shorten the path:** hire the critical scope; add helpers on lift days; order earlier; overlap envelope and rough-in after dry-in.

### 9.5 Build the procurement log
- **What:** a table: item, specification, supplier, lead time, need-by date, order-by date, ordered, confirmed delivery.
- **How:** order-by date = need-by date minus lead time minus a two-week margin.
- **Typical long-lead items [E]:** electrical service from the utility; trusses (3–8 weeks); windows and exterior doors (4–12 weeks); hold-down hardware and engineered lumber (1–4 weeks); heat pump equipment; cabinets (6–12 weeks); countertops (templated after cabinets, 2–3 weeks); special-order tile, plumbing fixtures, and appliances.
- **Standard:** confirm lead times by phone the week you build the log; they change.

### 9.6 Place long-lead orders
- **What:** confirmed orders for the items whose order-by date has arrived.
- **How:** order from the frozen plans and schedules only. Get written confirmations with sizes. Check rough opening sizes against the window order line by line.
- **Do now for later:** the utility service request, trusses, and windows are the three orders that most often stop a job.
- **If it goes wrong:** a window arrives the wrong size after framing. Reframing is cheaper than reordering; decide quickly.

### 9.7 Plan site logistics
- **What:** a site layout sketch: truck access and turnaround, material drop zones near where they are used, the crane or boom-truck position for trusses, storage, dumpster, toilet, temporary power, and concrete washout.
- **Standard:** keep everything off the septic drainfield and reserve area (11.7). Keep the drop zone for trusses flat and reachable by the delivery truck.
- **Do now for later:** deliveries should arrive just before use. Lumber stored for weeks warps and gets stolen.

### 9.8 Publish the baseline schedule
- **What:** a dated baseline shared with hired trades, the lender, and your household.
- **How:** give each trade their window and the conditions that must be true when they arrive. Give the lender the draw dates.
- **Standard:** save the baseline unchanged. All updates go in a working copy so you can compare later (21.2).

### 9.9 Run the weekly look-ahead
- **What:** a 30-minute weekly meeting producing a three-week plan.
- **How:** 1) What finished? 2) What is next for three weeks? 3) Which inspections to request? 4) Which orders are due? 5) Which trades to confirm? 6) What is the weather doing? 7) Update the finish date.
- **Standard:** call hired trades two weeks out, one week out, and the day before.
- **If it goes wrong:** the finish date slips every week. After three slips, re-plan: hire a scope or cut scope.

## Top mistakes
1. Scheduling at professional speed (9.2).
2. Ordering windows and trusses after framing starts (9.5, 9.6).
3. Missing the dry-in window (9.4).
4. No call-ahead routine for trades (9.9).
