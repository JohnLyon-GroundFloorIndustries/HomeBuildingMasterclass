# Module 4 — Design and Engineering

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not yet reviewed by a designer or engineer.** [V] verified, [E] estimate. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will have a permit-ready plan set, structural engineering, truss design, and energy code forms for a house that fits the lot, the budget, and your own skills.

**Before you start:** lot under contract or owned, survey, septic design location, setbacks and buffers drawn, budget ceiling.

**Principle:** you are the builder. Every line the designer draws is something you will cut, lift, and fasten. Simple shapes are the cheapest skill upgrade available.

---

### 4.1 Write the design brief
- **What:** one page: size, rooms, must-haves, budget for construction, and the skills limits from your audit.
- **How:** list rooms with rough sizes; set a hard area cap by dividing the construction budget by a local cost range you got in 1.8.
- **Track:** T2 writes the brief from comparable sales: bedroom and bath count, garage, lot fit. No personal features.
- **Output:** design brief.

### 4.2 Choose the design path
- **Options [E]:**

| Option | Cost | Time | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Stock plan, modified by a local drafter | Low | 4–8 weeks | Best value when a plan fits the lot |
| B. Residential designer, custom | Medium | 2–4 months | Awkward lots, specific needs |
| C. Architect | Highest | 3–6 months | Complex sites or high design ambition |
| D. Draft it yourself | Lowest cash | Long | Only with drafting experience; engineer still required |

- **Recommendation:** A or B. Either way hire someone who has had plans approved in your jurisdiction.
- **Who:** ask each candidate: "Which local engineer do you work with? How many plans have you permitted in this county in the last two years?"

### 4.3 Place the house on the site
- **What:** a scaled site concept showing house, driveway, septic tank, drainfield and reserve, well or water line, power route, and drainage direction.
- **How:** start from the constraints, not the house: setbacks, buffers, septic areas, and fire access first. Then orient for sun, view, and driveway slope.
- **Standard:** finished floor high enough that ground falls 6 in. in the first 10 ft from the house [V].
- **Do now for later:** this sketch becomes the building site plan in 5.6 and sets the top-of-foundation elevation in 12.6.
- **If it goes wrong:** the house only fits by entering the reserve area. Shrink or reshape the house; do not move the reserve without the septic designer.

### 4.4 Develop the floor plan
- **What:** a dimensioned floor plan that follows buildability rules.
- **Buildability rules [E]:**
    1. Rectangles and simple L-shapes. Each extra corner adds foundation, framing, roofing, and flashing work.
    2. Roof: one or two gable planes, common trusses at 24 in. on center, no hand-framed valleys if avoidable. Keep the pitch at 6 in 12 or lower so the family can sheath and dry it in on foot with fall protection; steeper roofs need roof jacks or staging and are a scope to hire.
    3. Dimension to 2-ft and 4-ft modules to suit sheet goods.
    4. Stack or group plumbing: kitchen, baths, and laundry on shared walls.
    5. Keep spans within standard truss and joist ranges.
    6. Standard window and door sizes from one manufacturer's stock list.
    7. A mechanical room or closet near the house center.
- **Standard:** bedroom egress windows, stair geometry, and hall widths meet code; your designer checks these.
- **Track:** T2 adds resale rules: primary bedroom with its own bath, walk-in pantry or equivalent storage, laundry room, and nothing unusual.

### 4.5 Choose foundation and structure
- **What:** foundation type and wall, floor, and roof systems.
- **Options (foundation) [E]:** crawl space (default in this region; easy plumbing access); slab-on-grade (cheaper, faster; plumbing is cast in place and unforgiving); basement (most cost and skill; only where the site calls for it).
- **Recommendation:** crawl space for a first build.
- **Standard:** the plans must show design criteria including snow load, wind, and seismic values [V]. Kitsap County Code 14.04 sets a 25 psf snow load with roof live load not reduced below 25 psf, 110 mph ultimate wind speed, Seismic Design Category D2, 12 in. frost depth, and moderate weathering [V]. Soil bearing is normally assumed at 1,500 psf unless a geotechnical report supports more [E]. A stock plan drawn for another region must be re-checked to these values, and a lot on or near a steep slope, on fill, or on soft or wet soil needs site-specific values from the engineer [E].
- **Point of no return:** foundation type is fixed once engineering starts.

### 4.6 Choose systems and the energy code path
- **What:** heating, water heating, and ventilation selections, and the list of energy credits you will claim.
- **How:** 1) Read the state energy code credit table with your designer. 2) Pick the heating system first; it sets how many more credits you need. 3) Choose remaining credits you can execute yourself: tighter air leakage, better windows, efficient water heating.
- **Standard:** under the 2021 state energy code a medium dwelling (most houses between 1,500 and 5,000 sq ft) must reach 8.0 credits [V]. Prescriptive insulation rose to R-60 ceilings and walls of R-20 plus R-5 continuous, or R-13 plus R-10 continuous [V].
- **Do now for later:** each credit is a promise the inspector checks. An air-leakage credit sets the blower door target you must hit in 19.2.
- **Options (heating) [E]:** ducted heat pump (common, needs duct design and testing); ductless heat pumps (simple, zone by zone); both are owner-installable only within licensing and refrigerant-handling limits.
- **If it goes wrong:** you claim a credit you cannot build. Changing credits after permit needs a revision.

### 4.7 Engage the structural engineer (wait)
- **What:** stamped structural sheets and calculations: foundation, shear walls, hold-downs, beams, headers.
- **How:** send the floor plan and elevations. Ask the engineer to design for owner construction: fewer hold-down types, common hardware, simple nailing schedules.
- **Standard:** the county accepts either engineered shear walls or prescriptive wall bracing, and a prescriptive plan must show the bracing method and the calculation of how much bracing each wall line needs and has [V]. In Seismic Design Category D2 a house falls outside the prescriptive rules quickly: irregular shapes and offsets, wall lines with too little solid wall beside large windows or a garage door, tall walls, engineered lumber beams or beams beyond the span tables, heavy point loads, and steep or filled sites [E]. Most houses hit at least one of these, so engineered lateral design is normal here [E]. Once plans are stamped, every size, nail, and connector on them is a requirement; a change goes back to the same engineer for revised stamped sheets and to the county as a revision [V].
- **Money out [E]:** a few thousand dollars for a simple house.
- **Before engineering starts:** ask the county and the engineer whether a soils report is needed. Slopes steeper than about 15 percent, mapped geologic hazard areas, fill, and soft soils commonly trigger one, and it sets bearing value, footing depth, and drainage [E].
- **Who, what to ask:** "Can we reduce the number of different shear wall types to two or three? Can hold-downs be one model?"

### 4.8 Order truss and floor designs (wait)
- **What:** sealed truss drawings and, if used, an engineered floor layout from the supplier.
- **How:** send plans to two truss suppliers; they design at no charge when quoting [E]. Confirm heel height for the attic insulation depth, and loads for any attic equipment.
- **Do now for later:** raised-heel trusses make room for full-depth insulation at the eaves.
- **Standard:** truss drawings go in the permit set and must be on site at framing inspection [E].

### 4.9 Complete the plan set
- **What:** site plan, foundation plan, floor plans, elevations, sections, framing plans, details, and schedules.
- **Standard:** the county publishes a list of required construction plan components; the foundation sheet must show a scaled layout with footing and wall dimensions [V].
- **How:** check the set against the county's components list line by line.

### 4.10 Complete energy code forms
- **What:** the energy compliance worksheet with credits listed, and heating sizing if required.
- **How:** the county's forms page lists an energy code worksheet [V]. Complete it with your designer or an energy rater.
- **Standard:** values on the form must match the plans and the products you will buy.

### 4.11 Review plans with the trades
- **What:** a marked-up set after a buildability review.
- **How:** walk the plans with a framer, plumber, electrician, and heating contractor, even where you will do the work. Ask each: "What here is hard, slow, or likely to fail inspection?"
- **Money out:** pay for an hour of each trade's time.
- **Done when:** each comment is accepted or rejected in writing.

### 4.12 Freeze the design
- **What:** a signed, dated final set.
- **Standard:** after this date, a change needs a written change order with cost and time, and after permit, a revision.
- **Done when:** you both sign the cover sheet.

## Module gate
- [ ] Plans match the site concept and all setbacks
- [ ] Structural sheets and truss drawings in hand
- [ ] Energy credits chosen and achievable by you
- [ ] Trade review comments resolved
- [ ] Design frozen

## Top mistakes
1. Complex roofs and footprints that double the labor (4.4).
2. Designing past the budget and hoping bids come in low (4.1).
3. Claiming energy credits without a plan to build them (4.6).
4. Changing the design after freeze (4.12).
