# Module 10 — Managing the Trades

**Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not legal advice; not yet reviewed by an attorney or builder.** [V] verified, [E] estimate. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

**Outcome:** when you finish this module you will be able to hire, contract, schedule, check, and pay any trade, with no surprise liens and no scope arguments.

**Before you start:** legal scope matrix (Module 2), plans, budget, schedule, attorney-reviewed subcontract template (2.3).

**Why you still need this as a DIY family:** even on T1 you will likely hire septic design, engineering, concrete pumping, possibly finishing, heat pump commissioning, drywall finishing, countertops, and any licensed work you choose not to do. On T2, electrical and plumbing are hired by law.

---

### 10.1 Decide which scopes to hire
- **What:** a final list of hired scopes.
- **How:** for each scope ask four questions: Does the law require a license? Is a mistake dangerous or irreversible? Is it on the critical path during a weather window? Is my hourly labor value low here (1.5)?
- **Standard:** one yes on the first two means hire. Two yeses on the last two means hire.

### 10.2 Write each scope of work
- **What:** a one- to two-page scope per trade.
- **Contents:** plans and sheets it covers; exactly what is included; what is excluded; who supplies materials; who pulls the permit and calls inspections; site conditions you will provide (access, power, water, cleared area); schedule window; cleanup; warranty.
- **Standard:** if a task sits between two trades, name who does it. Classic gaps [E]: who cuts and patches for whom, who supplies and installs bath fans, who seals penetrations, who hauls trash.
- **Done when:** someone outside the project can read it and say what is not included.

### 10.3 Build and verify the bidder list
- **What:** three candidates per scope, each verified.
- **How:** ask suppliers, inspectors' counter staff, neighbors mid-build, and other trades. Check each on the state Verify tool for active registration, bond, insurance, and any actions.
- **Standard:** registered contractors must be bonded and insured [V].

### 10.4 Issue bid packages
- **What:** the same package to every bidder: plans, scope, schedule window, bid due date, and a bid form.
- **How:** ask for a fixed price with a breakdown, unit prices for likely extras, exclusions listed, and how long the price holds.
- **Standard:** walk the site with each bidder.

### 10.5 Level the bids
- **What:** a comparison sheet where every bid covers the same work.
- **How:** list scope items down the side, bidders across the top; mark included, excluded, or unclear; price the gaps; then compare totals.
- **If it goes wrong:** one bid is far lower. It usually excludes something or misread the plans. Ask before celebrating.

### 10.6 Check references and select
- **What:** a chosen trade for each scope.
- **How:** call two recent customers and one supplier. Ask: showed up when promised? Passed inspections first time? Any liens or payment disputes? Would you hire again?
- **Standard:** reliability outranks price for anything on the critical path.

### 10.7 Sign the subcontract
- **What:** a signed agreement before any work.
- **Contents:** scope by reference; fixed price; payment schedule tied to completed and inspected milestones; change orders in writing before the work; insurance and registration requirements; lien waiver with each payment; schedule and notice terms; warranty; dispute process.
- **Standard:** on residential jobs of four units or fewer with a price of $1,000 or more, a contractor must give the customer a written disclosure statement before starting work. It gives the registration number and bond amount, warns that the property may be liened, and says the customer may withhold a contractually defined percentage as retainage [V]. A contractor who did not give it cannot bring a lien claim on that contract [V]. Expect it, sign it, and keep a copy. It is not required between contractors, so a T2 family building under its own registration should not count on receiving one [V].
- **Standard (registration):** hire only registered contractors, and re-check on the start date. An unregistered contractor cannot sue you to collect and generally cannot claim a lien [V], but you lose the bond and the insurance, and you may be treated as the employer of its workers (8.5) [E].
- **Standard (payments) [E]:** no large deposits. Pay for special-order materials on delivery to site, in your name where possible.

### 10.8 Log lien notices
- **What:** a log of every notice of the right to claim a lien you receive.
- **Why:** suppliers and subcontractors who are not paid can lien your property even if you paid the contractor who hired them [V].
- **Standard:** no notice is required from anyone who contracts directly with you, from a subcontractor hired directly by one of your trades, or from a laborer claiming only for labor. As your own general contractor you hire most trades and buy most materials directly, so most of the people who could lien your house will never send you a notice [V]. Others who furnish materials, equipment, or professional services without a contract with you must send a Notice to Owner; for a new single-family home it protects only what they furnished from ten days before the notice onward [V]. A lien must be recorded within 90 days after the claimant last furnished labor, services, materials, or equipment. A suit to enforce it must be filed within eight calendar months after recording and served on the owner within 90 days after filing [V].
- **How:** each notice names someone who must be paid. Before paying the contractor, get a waiver from that sender too (10.10). Because many claimants need not send a notice, keep a second list: every trade and supplier you pay directly, and, from each trade, the names of its subcontractors and main suppliers. Collect waivers against that list, not only against the notice log.

### 10.9 Schedule and confirm site readiness
- **What:** the trade arrives to a site that is ready.
- **How:** send the readiness checklist with the two-week call: previous work complete and inspected, access clear, materials on site, power and water available, plans and embed or layout marks in place.
- **Standard:** a trade sent home once will put you at the back of their list.

### 10.10 Inspect, collect the waiver, then pay
- **What:** payment made only after three checks.
- **How:** 1) Walk the work against the scope and plans; list defects in writing. 2) Confirm the required inspection passed. 3) Collect a signed lien waiver for the amount being paid, from the trade and from anyone who sent a notice. Then pay promptly.
- **Options (waivers):** conditional waiver on progress payment (effective once the check clears) and unconditional waiver for the prior payment; conditional final waiver at final payment [E]. Use the forms your attorney approved.
- **Standard:** hold the last 5–10% until punch items are done [E], if the contract says so. On private residential work the right to hold money back comes from the contract [E]; the state's required disclosure statement itself tells owners they may withhold a contractually defined percentage as retainage [V]. Write the percentage and the release condition into the subcontract.
- **Standard (releases):** a claimant who has been paid and has accepted the payment must, when you ask, sign and deliver a release of lien rights for that payment [V].
- **If it goes wrong:** a lien is recorded. Call the attorney the same day; the deadlines above are short. The owner's tools include withholding the claimed amount from the trade responsible while a lien suit is pending, recording a surety bond in place of the lien so a loan draw or sale can proceed, and a fast court motion to release a lien that is frivolous or clearly excessive, in which the losing side pays the other's attorney fees [V].

### 10.11 Close out each trade
- **What:** final waiver, warranty in writing, as-built notes, manuals, and a scorecard entry.
- **Output:** trade file complete; scorecard used in 21.3.

## Top mistakes
1. Vague scopes and verbal changes (10.2, 10.7).
2. Paying ahead of the work (10.7, 10.10).
3. Relying on the notice log alone, when most claimants never have to send a notice (10.8).
4. Calling a trade to a site that is not ready (10.9).

## Call script — bidder
"I'm an owner-builder with an issued permit for a [size] house at [location]. I need a fixed-price bid for [scope], with plans and a written scope attached. Work window is [dates]. Can you walk the site this week, and are your registration and insurance current?"
