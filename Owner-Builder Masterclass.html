    Assumptions the course is built on

    • About 2,000 sq ft, single story, wood frame on a crawl space, all-electric with a heat pump, composition roof, fiber-cement siding.
    • Unincorporated Kitsap County, Washington, on septic.
    • Both tracks: owner-occupied (T1) and spec (T2), marked in every module.
    • Slab, basement, sewer, and city-lot variants are noted where they change a step but are not written out.

    How to read it

    • Every step has a number. Work them in order; a step never depends on a later one.
    • V marks a fact checked against a cited source on 15 Nov 2026. E marks an estimate or trade practice that was not sourced.
    • T1 is the owner-occupied track. T2 is the spec track.
    • A step marked wait cannot finish until an outside party acts: an inspector, a lender, a supplier.
    • Each module ends with a gate. Tick the boxes as you go; this page remembers them in your browser only.

    How it was made, and what that means

    • Each module was written from the step map, then reviewed from the viewpoint of a builder, an inspector, and a lender, attorney, or trade as relevant. The review notes in each module list what changed.
    • That first review was done by the same AI author in the same working session.
    • A second, independent round followed on 15 Nov 2026. Five AI reviewers, standing in for a builder, a structural engineer, a county inspector, a Washington construction attorney, and a CPA, each checked their modules against current statutes, codes, and agency sources without seeing the author's reasoning. They proposed 125 corrections; 123 were applied and 2 were dropped as duplicates. Their largest corrections: the owner exemption from contractor registration was misread (work done to sell is never exempt, whatever the holding period); code minimums had been presented as the build standard for anchor bolts and reinforcing; the blower door limit is 4.0, not 5; every ducted system is leak-tested; sales tax applies on both tracks; and the federal tax placeholders were replaced with sourced rules.
    • Five steps were added after that round, at the reviewers' request: the temporary power inspection (11.5), crawl space water (12.21), the garage slab (12.24), scaffold and staging (13.15), and eave and rake framing (13.16). They were written by the author and have not been through a review round; their standards are marked E until sourced. Later steps in Modules 11–13 were renumbered.
    • Neither round is a professional review. AI reviewers can misread a statute or a code table too. A licensed builder, engineer, attorney, and CPA still need to check the parts that are theirs.

    Known limits

    1. Code edition. Washington is on the 2021 residential, energy, and plumbing codes, with the 2024 codes scheduled for 3 May 2027; the electrical code is the 2023 edition, with the 2026 edition from 31 December 2026. Several county checklists the course cites are still written to the 2018 codes. The inspector review re-checked the main energy, plumbing, and inspection values against the codes in force, but not every checklist-sourced number.
    2. Electrical. Module 15 gives sequence and coordination, not wiring rules. A self-wiring owner needs the adopted electrical code or a paid consult.
    3. Craft tolerances, durations, and costs are estimates throughout.
    4. Financing (Module 7) is general practice; lender rules vary bank by bank and none were verified.
    5. Disclosure and warranty law on sale, and all federal tax, were deliberately left as questions for the attorney and CPA.
    6. Now sourced by the review round grounding electrode inspection (12.10), owner septic installation (11.11), the plumbing owner exemption (2.1), occupancy approval (19.9), and Kitsap's design criteria (4.5). Still not verified: utility trench specifications (11.13); whether one house on an existing lot is exempt from concurrency (5.8); whether an entity such as an LLC can use the owner exemptions; whether helpers may assist on owner electrical work; whether roof nailing is inspected before underlayment; and the Veterans Affairs handbook text on veterans acting as their own builder, which was read from third-party copies.
    7. Sources that could not be opened the county framing and underfloor checklists. Two reviewers reported that some county checklist links in the Sources lists returned "file not found" when opened directly; re-check each link before relying on it.
    8. Variants not written slab and basement foundations, prescriptive wall bracing (13.9, 13.12), exterior continuous insulation details, two-story framing, gas appliances, sewer connection, city permitting.
    9. Not yet produced the templates each module names (worksheets, bid forms, checklists), and any filmed demonstrations.

    Draft v1 — 15 Nov 2026 — not yet reviewed by a professional. V verified, E estimate. T1 owner-occupied, T2 spec.

    Outcome: when you finish this module you will have a one-page decision memo: build or don't, which track, the budget ceiling, and the hours you are committing.

    Before you start: 12 months of household finances, a list of your tools and equipment, your work and family calendar for the next two years.

    Why it comes first: every later module spends money. This one costs a weekend and three phone calls.

    1.1Define the finished result

    • What one paragraph stating what you are building, where, for whom, and by when.
    • How write size range, bedrooms and baths, area, target move-in or sale date, and the one thing that would make the project a failure.
    • Standard specific enough that a stranger could tell whether you hit it.
    • Done when both adults in the household have signed the paragraph.
    • Output project statement, first page of the binder.

    1.2Audit skills and equipment

    • What a table of every trade scope scored for your ability and equipment.
    • How 1) List the scopes from the DIY matrix in the course outline. 2) Score each: done it at this scale, done it small, never done it. 3) Mark the equipment you own, can rent, or lack. 4) Mark who in the family does it and who is the second pair of hands.
    • Standard "done it small" counts as never for foundations, roofing, and anything inspected for structure.
    • If it goes wrong the audit is flattering. Have someone who has worked beside you score it too.
    • Output skills and equipment audit.

    1.3Budget your time

    • What hours per week you can give, for how many months, with known gaps.
    • How 1) Count realistic weekly hours after work, family, and rest. 2) Block out known absences. 3) Multiply by months. 4) Compare to a rough labor need of 2,500–4,000 owner hours for a house this size E. The low end holds only if concrete finishing, roofing, drywall finishing, and most mechanical work are hired; one reviewer judged even that optimistic, so plan on the upper half.
    • Standard two people at 20 hours a week each give about 2,000 hours a year. If the math needs more than two years, plan to hire more.
    • If it goes wrong the schedule depends on a single person. Name the scopes you will hire if that person is unavailable for 60 days.
    • Output time budget.

    1.4Set the budget ceiling

    • What the most you will spend, and the cash you can put in.
    • How 1) Total liquid cash you will commit. 2) Estimate borrowing capacity (confirmed in Module 7). 3) Subtract a household reserve of at least six months of expenses E. 4) The remainder is the ceiling for land, soft costs, construction, and contingency together.
    • Standard the ceiling does not move later because the plans grew.
    • Output budget ceiling, signed.

    1.5Price your own labor

    • What an honest estimate of what your labor saves.
    • How 1) For each scope you will do, find a rough hired price (two phone quotes or a local builder's ranges). 2) Subtract materials. 3) The difference is labor value. 4) Divide by your estimated hours for an hourly figure.
    • Standard savings are commonly 15–30% of construction cost for families who self-perform most non-licensed work E. As a cross-check, builders in a 2024 national survey reported overhead of 5.7% and profit of 11.0% of sale price against construction cost of 64.4%, so the general contractor's share alone is about a quarter of construction cost on a house built for sale V. Treat anything above 30% as a warning. Labor savings on your own home are not taxed, so compare your hourly figure with your take-home pay, not your gross pay E.
    • If it goes wrong the hourly figure is below what you earn at work. That is information, not failure; some scopes are better hired.
    • Output labor value table.

    1.6Test the owner-occupied numbers T1

    • What a comparison of building against buying an equivalent house.
    • How total cost to build (land, site, fees, construction, financing, contingency) against the price of a comparable existing or new home nearby.
    • Standard include what you will pay to live elsewhere during the build; sales tax on every material and on the full price of every hired contractor, 9.2% in unincorporated Kitsap County in late 2026 V (many bids are quoted before tax); loan fees and interest; and property tax during the build. Compare against the full cost of buying, including a buyer's closing costs E.
    • Done when you can state the dollar advantage or disadvantage in one sentence.
    • Output build-versus-buy sheet.

    1.7Test the spec numbers T2

    • What a one-page pro forma showing profit after every cost.
    • How expected sale price from three recent comparable sales, minus land, site, fees, construction, financing interest, insurance, sales costs, excise tax, and income tax. Then test two bad cases: price 10% lower, and sale six months later.
    • Standard the state excise tax on a sale is 1.1% of the price up to $525,000 and 1.28% of the part above that up to $1,525,000 through 2026; from 1 Jan 2027 those thresholds are $551,000 and $1,551,000 V. Kitsap County adds 0.5% V. On a $700,000 sale in 2027 the total is about $11,470. Agent commissions and closing costs are in addition E. Construction cost must include sales tax on materials and on hired contractors' full charges; a spec builder cannot recover it V. For income tax, plan on the profit being ordinary income and, if the build is a business, subject to 15.3% self-employment tax as well V. Use the CPA's combined rate, not a guess.
    • Track T2 work on a house built for sale is limited by contractor and trade licensing law. Do not finalize this sheet until Module 2.
    • If it goes wrong profit disappears in either bad case. The margin is too thin for a first project.
    • Output spec pro forma with two stress cases.

    1.8Run three reality-check conversations wait

    • What notes from a lender, a builder, and a real estate agent.
    • How book 30–60 minutes each. Bring the project statement and your numbers.
    • Who and what to ask
      • Lender: "Do you lend to owner-builders acting as their own general contractor? What equity, reserves, and experience do you require?"
      • Builder: "What do first-time owner-builders get wrong here? What would this house cost you to build?"
      • Agent: "What sells fastest in this area and at what price? What do buyers reject?"
    • Money out offer to pay the builder for the hour.
    • Done when each conversation has changed or confirmed at least one number.
    • Output three sets of notes.

    1.9Decide and write the memo

    • What a one-page go/no-go memo.
    • How state the decision, track, ceiling, hours, top three risks, and the conditions under which you will stop.
    • Options go on T1; go on T2; go on T1 now and T2 later; no-go; delay 6–12 months to build cash or skills.
    • Recommendation T1 first for most families. It allows the most owner labor under Washington law and has the most forgiving financing.
    • Done when both adults sign.
    • Output decision memo.

    Outcome: when you finish this module you will have a signed matrix of who may legally perform each scope on your project, an ownership structure, and a tax plan for your track.

    Before you start: the decision memo from Module 1.

    Why it matters: in Washington the two tracks are legally different projects. Getting this wrong can void permits, trigger penalties, and expose you on sworn affidavits.

    2.1Map who may legally do each scope

    • What a matrix of every scope with three columns: owner may do it, registered contractor required, licensed trade required.
    • How fill it from the rules below, then mark each V item with the statute to show the attorney.
    • Rules
      • General construction: a person working on their own property is exempt from contractor registration, but not when the work is done for the purpose of selling or leasing the property. That purpose test has no time limit V. Separately, anyone who offers a property for sale without having occupied or used the structure for more than one year after substantial completion is defined as a contractor, unless they contracted with a registered general contractor and did not superintend the work V. The limit for property owned under twelve months is a different rule: it applies to an owner who hires a registered contractor to run the project V.
      • Electrical: an owner may wire their own property unless it is a new building intended for rent, sale, or lease, or is offered for sale within twelve months of obtaining it V. The state's owner affidavit for a new single-family home is made under penalty of perjury and says three things: the house is not intended for rent, sale, or lease; you will live there at least 24 months after completing the electrical work; and you will do the electrical work personally V. The statute separately says a householder may receive help from a friend, neighbor, or relative who is not in the electrical trade V. Ask the state electrical inspector how that fits with "personally" before anyone else pulls wire.
      • Plumbing: no plumber certificate is required to do plumbing at your own residence or on other property you own, and a householder may receive help from a friend, neighbor, or relative who is not in the plumbing trade V. The plumbing contractor license rule exempts a person contracting for plumbing work on their own residence, but not on a building that is for rent, sale, or lease V. The text does not settle whether an owner may personally plumb a house built for sale. Treat T2 plumbing as hired work unless the department or your attorney says otherwise in writing E.
      • Low-voltage: an owner may do telecommunications work on their own property without a telecommunications contractor license, except on a new building intended for rent, sale, or lease, or on property offered for sale within twelve months of obtaining it V.
    • Output legal scope matrix, draft.

    2.2Decide your occupancy commitment

    • What a written statement of how long you will live in the house.
    • How read three rules against your real plans, including a possible job move: the electrical affidavit (live there 24 months after the electrical work); the contractor definition (offering the house for sale before you have occupied it more than one year after substantial completion); and the purpose test (work done in order to sell is not exempt, however long you hold) V. Write down now why you are building. If you later have to sell early, what you intended when you did the work is the fact that matters, and it depends on evidence E.
    • Standard the electrical affidavit is a sworn statement. Sign it only if the 24 months is realistic.
    • Track T1 only. T2 does not sign owner-occupancy affidavits.
    • If it goes wrong you may need to sell early. Ask the attorney in 2.3 what that exposes you to before you sign anything.
    • Output occupancy statement.

    2.4Choose the contractor path T2

    • What a decision on how the spec build is lawfully performed.
    • Options
    Option What it means Cost and effort Risk
    A. Register as a general contractor You build under your own registration; licensed trades still do electrical and plumbing Bond, insurance, fee; days to weeks You carry contractor liability
    B. Hire a registered general contractor They run the job; your labor is limited Their fee, often 10–20% E Lowest legal risk; lowest sweat equity
    C. Build it as your home, live in it, sell later T1 rules apply Time Not a spec path. If the purpose of the build is to sell, the owner exemption does not apply however long you hold V
    D. Do not do a spec build yet — — —
    • Recommendation A for a family that intends to build more than once; C only if the house is truly being built as your home. Confirm with the attorney.
    • If it goes wrong working as a contractor without registration is a gross misdemeanor and also an infraction with a penalty of $1,200 to $10,000, and each day and each worksite is a separate infraction V. A building permit obtained by falsifying information to claim an exemption is forfeited V. An unregistered contractor cannot sue to collect for the work V.
    • Output decision recorded in the matrix.

    2.5Register as a contractor T2 · if chosen wait

    • What an active registration with the Department of Labor & Industries.
    • How 1) Register the business with the Department of Revenue. 2) Buy a continuous surety bond. 3) Buy general liability insurance naming the department as certificate holder. 4) Submit the notarized application and fee V.
    • Standard general contractor bond $30,000 since 1 July 2024; liability insurance of $200,000 public liability and $50,000 property damage, or $250,000 combined V. No exam V.
    • Who insurance agent for bond and policy; local department office can notarize.
    • Done when your registration shows active on the department's Verify tool.
    • If it goes wrong a bond or policy lapse suspends the registration. Calendar renewals.

    2.6Choose the ownership structure

    • What a decision on who holds title: yourselves, or an entity.
    • Options individual ownership (simplest; required in practice for most owner-occupied loans E; the state's electrical affidavit for a new single-family home is a personal statement that you will live there and do the work yourself, which a company cannot make V); a limited liability company (common for spec; affects financing and insurance). If a company holds title, the company is the owner. For a spec build the company is then the party that registers as the contractor, and whether its members may personally use any owner exemption depends on facts the attorney must review E.
    • Standard (state) to be taxed as a speculative builder, the same legal person must own the land and do the building. The Department of Revenue looks past title to who intended to own, who paid for the land, who paid for the improvements, and how lenders dealt with it V. A company that builds on land owned by its members, officers, or partners is taxed as a contractor making a retail sale, not as a speculative builder: it owes retailing business tax and must collect sales tax on the construction, labor included V. Expect the same result if you own the land personally and build through your own separately registered company E. Put the land, the contractor registration, the material purchases, and the labor in one name.
    • Standard (federal) an LLC with one member is ignored for income tax unless it elects otherwise, so the house is reported on the owner's own return; an LLC with two or more members is a partnership and files its own return V. Either can elect corporate or S corporation treatment. The entity does not change whether spec profit is ordinary income. It can change self-employment tax, payroll filings, and whether the home-sale exclusion is available if you later move in. Ask the CPA how a married couple's LLC is classified in a community property state, and whether an S corporation is worth its cost for one house E.
    • Who attorney and CPA together.
    • Done when the structure is chosen before any purchase offer.
    • If it goes wrong title vests in the wrong name. Fixing it later costs fees and may trigger tax.

    2.7Meet a CPA wait

    • What a written tax plan for your track.
    • How ask:
      1. "T1: what records do I need to establish my cost basis, and what are the rules for excluding gain when I eventually sell my home?"
      2. "T2: on our facts (first house, our other income, how we hold title), is the profit ordinary income from a business, and is it subject to self-employment tax?"
      3. "T2: confirm speculative builder treatment for state sales and business taxes, and tell us whether we must register with the Department of Revenue."
      4. "Which costs are added to the cost of the house, may we deduct or must we capitalize construction interest and property tax, and how should we track them?"
      5. "T2: what estimated tax payments are due, and when, in the year of sale?"
      6. "If we rent the house or move in instead of selling, or must sell a house we built to live in sooner than planned, what changes?"
    • Standard (state, T2) a speculative builder pays sales tax on all materials, tools, and supplies, and on the full charge of every contractor it hires, labor included; it may not use a reseller permit for them V. It does not owe business tax or collect sales tax on the value of its own construction, and the sale of the finished real estate is exempt from business tax and is not a retail sale V. Anything bought without sales tax, such as some online or out-of-state orders, owes use tax at the job-site rate V. If the buyer gets title or an immediate right to possession while the house is under construction, work after that date is a retail sale: you owe retailing business tax and collect sales tax on that part of the price V. A purchase agreement that closes after completion does not by itself do this V.
    • Standard (state, T1) an owner building their own home is a consumer. You pay sales tax on materials, tools, and rentals, and every contractor you hire must charge sales tax on its full price, labor and materials V. Use tax applies to untaxed purchases V. Building or later selling your own home creates no business tax and no registration duty E. The combined rate in unincorporated Kitsap County is 9.2% in late 2026; check the rate for the delivery address V.
    • Standard (state registration, T2) you must register with the Department of Revenue if you have $12,000 or more a year of gross income taxable under the business tax, must collect or pay any tax to the department, or need any other state business license V. Real estate sale proceeds are exempt, so for most spec builders the trigger is the contractor registration (2.5), forming an LLC, or a sale during construction E. Ask the CPA to confirm for your structure.
    • Standard (federal, T1) your basis is what the house cost you in money: land, materials, paid labor, permits, design, utility connections, and the sales tax paid on them. The value of your own labor, and of unpaid help, is not part of basis V. When you sell a main home you can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned it and lived in it for two of the five years before the sale V. The two years of use start when you move in, not when you buy the land E. A sale sooner than that because of a job move of 50 miles or more, a health reason, or an unforeseeable event can qualify for a prorated exclusion V. A service member on qualified extended duty can elect to suspend the five-year period for up to ten years, but two years of actual use are still required V. Interest on a construction loan can be treated as home mortgage interest for up to 24 months if the house becomes your home when it is ready V. Building, living in briefly, and selling repeatedly can make you a dealer; ask the CPA before the second house E.
    • Standard (federal, T2) a house built to sell is property held for sale to customers, not a capital asset, so profit is ordinary income whatever the holding period V. If the activity is a trade or business the profit is also subject to self-employment tax of 15.3%, with the 12.4% Social Security part ending at an annual earnings cap V. Whether one house is a trade or business depends on your facts. Costs of building are added to the cost of the house and recovered at sale, not deducted as you spend V. Most small builders are exempt from the uniform capitalization rules, and a taxpayer may elect to capitalize taxes and carrying charges V; which treatment of construction interest and property tax applies to you is a CPA question. Your own labor is not a cost and is never deducted; it shows up as profit V. A sale puts the whole tax bill in one year: estimated payments are generally required if you will owe $1,000 or more V. If you pay an unincorporated trade $2,000 or more in a year (for payments from 2026) in the course of the business, collect a Form W-9 before paying and file Form 1099-NEC V. Homeowners paying for work on their own home do not file these V.
    • Standard (federal, credits) the homeowner credits for efficiency improvements and for solar or geothermal ended for property placed in service after 31 December 2025, and the builder credit for efficient new homes applies only to homes acquired through 30 June 2026 V. Do not put any of them in a budget. State and utility rebates are separate; check them directly.

    6.3Price materials

    6.4Get bids for hired scopes wait

    • What at least two written bids for each scope you will hire.

    6.5Add soft costs

    6.6Add carrying costs

    • What costs that accrue with time: loan interest, property tax, insurance, utilities, and your current housing if it overlaps.
    • How multiply monthly cost by the schedule in months, then add three months E.     • Track T2 carries through the sale closing, not just completion.

    6.7Value owner labor and set contingency

    • What a contingency line, and a record of what your labor is replacing.
    • How 1) For each self-performed scope, record the fallback bid from 6.4. 2) Set contingency as a percentage of hard costs.

    • What one sheet: code, description, quantity, unit cost, total, source (quote, bid, estimate), and date.

    6.9Cut cost if over the ceiling

    • If it goes wrong you cut contingency to make it fit. That is not a cut; it is a deferred overrun.

    6.10Set up weekly cost tracking

    • What a weekly routine and report.

    • Crew, time 1 person, 1 hour a week.
    • Output weekly cost report; change log.

    • What coverage or a clear rule for every non-contractor who works on site.
    • Standard do not assume a homeowner exemption for building a new house.     8.6Write the site safety plan

    • What two pages covering the hazards that kill and injure on residential sites.

      2.       4. Power tools: guards on, eye and hearing protection, no cords in water. Framing nailers set to sequential (single-shot) trigger, not bump-fire; free hand kept out of the line of the nail; air disconnected before clearing a jam E.

    Assumed build

    • Unincorporated Kitsap County, permit issued, plans approved.

    • Two pours: footings first, walls second.
    • Gently sloping lot, no geotechnical conditions, septic and utilities already located (Module 11).

    12.1Review the foundation plan against every sheet

    • What a marked-up foundation plan with every conflict found and resolved before any dirt moves.
    • How 1) Overlay the foundation plan on the floor framing, plumbing, and electrical plans. 2) Check that beam pockets, pier pads, and bearing walls line up with framing above. 3) Check overall dimensions add up along each wall. 4) List questions and send them to the designer or engineer in one message.
    • Standard zero unresolved conflicts (craft).
    • Crew, time 1 person, 2–4 hours.
    • Output marked-up plan and the engineer's written answers.
    • Done when every dimension string closes and every question has a written answer.
    • If it goes wrong a conflict found later costs a plan revision. A change to the approved plans needs a permit revision.

    12.2Build the embed and penetration schedule

    • What one list of everything that must be cast into or pass through the concrete.
    • How walk each trade's plan and list item, location, size, and height: anchor bolts, hold-downs, plumbing sleeves (water in, sewer out), electrical conduit sleeves, grounding electrode, crawl space vents, access opening (at least 16 in. x 24 in. through a wall, 18 in. x 24 in. through the floor V), beam pockets (beam width plus 1/2 in. air space each side and at the end, bearing height taken from the framing section E), a low-point drain sleeve through the footing or wall where the crawl space floor will sit below outside grade (12.20), crossings for the footing drain outfall and downspout lines, hose-bib and gas sleeves, radon provisions if applicable.
    • Do now for later this list is the step. Ask the plumber and electrician to mark it up, even if you will do that work yourself.
    • Inspector checks sleeves for plumbing and electrical through the wall or footing V.
    • Local note state radon construction requirements apply in seven listed counties; Kitsap is not on the list V. Confirm whether an unvented crawl space design triggers them.
    • Crew, time 1 person, 2–3 hours plus trade input.
    • Output embed schedule in the binder; copy taped inside the plan set.
    • Done when each trade has initialed the list.
    • If it goes wrong a missed sleeve means core drilling cured concrete, and possibly an engineer's approval for the hole.

    12.3Choose the forming and pour method

    • What a written decision on who forms and pours, and how.
    • Options E
    Option Cost Time Skill Risk
    A. Hire a foundation subcontractor for all of it Highest 1–2 weeks Low Low; schedule depends on the sub
    B. You form and place steel; hire a finisher and pump for pour days Medium 3–5 weeks part-time Medium Medium
    C. Do it all with rented form panels Lower 4–6 weeks part-time High Blowouts, out-of-level walls
    D. Insulated concrete forms Higher material 3–5 weeks Medium Bracing and consolidation

    • Track note on a house built for sale, your own labor falls under the contractor rules in Module 2. Decide A unless you are registered.
    • Done when method chosen, quotes in hand for the hired parts.

    12.4Order materials and book pour-day services

    • What confirmed orders and dates for rebar, forms, hardware, concrete, and pump.
    • How 1) Take off rebar lengths, add laps and about 10% waste E. 2) Order hold-downs and anchor bolts by the exact model on the plans. 3) Reserve form panels. 4) Open an account with a ready-mix supplier; give them the mix required by the plans. 5) Book the pump tentatively for both pours.
    • Standard concrete strength and air content per plans. Washington checklists call for at least 3,000 psi and 5–7% air entrainment for walls exposed to weather V. That is a durability floor, not your design: order the strength, slump, and air content written in the structural notes, and keep every delivery ticket. If the notes call for concrete strength testing or special inspection, book the testing agency before you book the truck E. Do not let anyone add water at the site beyond what the ticket allows; it lowers strength E.
    • Money out hardware and rebar now; concrete and pump on delivery.
    • Who ready-mix dispatcher. Ask: minimum load, short-load fee, lead time, Saturday delivery, washout rules.
    • Done when every order has a confirmation and a date.
    • If it goes wrong special-order hold-downs arrive late. Order them first.

    12.5Establish layout lines

    • What batter boards and string lines marking the outside face of the foundation.
    • Options A. Surveyor stakes the building corners (few hundred dollars and up E, lowest risk). B. You lay out from surveyed property corners with a total station or tapes. Recommended: A when any wall is within a few feet of a setback or buffer; B otherwise, checked by diagonals.
    • How 1) Set batter boards 4–6 ft outside each corner. 2) Pull strings for outside wall faces. 3) Measure both diagonals. 4) Adjust until equal.
    • Standard diagonals within 1/4 in.; wall lengths within 1/8 in. (craft) E.
    • Crew, time, tools 2 people, half a day; builder's level or laser, 100-ft tape, stakes.
    • Done when diagonals match and each string is labeled.
    • If it goes wrong strings get knocked. Mark their positions on the batter boards with saw kerfs so they can be reset.

    12.6Verify setbacks and set the top-of-foundation elevation

    • What measured setbacks recorded, and one benchmark elevation for the top of wall.
    • How 1) Measure from property lines, easements, and buffers to the strings; compare to the approved site plan. 2) Pick top-of-wall height so finished grade can slope away, the sewer or septic line has fall, and the driveway works. 3) Mark a benchmark on a stake or tree outside the work area.
    • Standard top of wall at least 6 in. above finished grade, 4 in. with masonry veneer V. Ground falls at least 6 in. in the first 10 ft from the foundation V.
    • Inspector checks footings and foundation outside setbacks, critical areas, and easements V.
    • Do now for later this elevation sets floor height, step count at every door, and garage slab height.
    • Done when setbacks are written on the site plan and the benchmark is marked.
    • If it goes wrong foundation set too low is the classic drainage failure and cannot be fixed after the pour.

    12.7Excavate footings to bearing soil

    • What trenches at plan depth and width on firm, undisturbed soil.
    • How 1) Dig to grade using the laser. 2) Hand-clean the bottom. 3) Step the trench where the ground slopes. 4) Dig interior pier pads.
    • Standard bottom level, or stepped where the ground slopes more than 1 ft in 10 ft V. Depth at or below frost depth per plans.
    • Inspector checks subgrade firm and undisturbed; loose soil, mud, water, and organics removed. Over-excavated or filled areas need a compaction report from a special inspector or geotechnical engineer V.
    • Weather and safety call for utility locates before digging; keep spoils back from trench edges; pump out standing water.
    • Crew, time, tools 1 operator and 1 grade checker, 1–2 days; excavator with a smooth bucket, laser.
    • Done when every trench bottom reads on grade and is clean.
    • If it goes wrong you dig too deep. Do not refill with loose soil. Fill the extra depth with concrete, or get the engineer's direction.

    12.8Form the footings

    • What forms set to line and level at plan width and thickness.
    • How stake 2x form boards to the strings, set tops to the benchmark, brace every 2–3 ft, add step bulkheads.
    • Standard minimum 12 in. wide and 6 in. thick, or as the plans require; footing projects at least 2 in. past the wall face and no more than its own thickness V. Form tops level within 1/4 in. (craft) E.
    • Crew, time 2 people, 1–2 days.
    • Pier pads form the interior pier pads at the same time. Their size, thickness, and reinforcing come from the point loads on the plans, not a generic square. Set the tops to the beam and girder heights and place any cast-in post bases now E.
    • Done when laser reads level around the full perimeter and diagonals still match.

    12.9Place footing rebar and vertical dowels

    • What all footing steel tied in position, with dowels standing where the walls will be.
    • How 1) Set horizontal bars on chairs or dobies. 2) Tie laps. 3) Tie hooked vertical dowels at plan spacing along the wall centerline. 4) Cap exposed bar ends.
    • Standard cover of 3 in. where concrete is cast against earth; 1-1/2 in. for #5 and smaller bars exposed to earth or weather; bend diameter at least 6 bar diameters; two ties per lap; bars clean V. Kitsap is Seismic Design Category D2, where the code requires reinforcing in footings and stem walls. The prescriptive minimum is one No. 4 horizontal bar 3 to 4 in. above the footing bottom, one No. 4 horizontal bar within 12 in. of the top of the stem wall, and No. 4 vertical dowels no more than 48 in. apart, each with a standard hook in the footing, 3 in. clear of the footing bottom, and reaching at least 14 in. up into the stem wall V. That is the code floor, not a design: stamped plans usually show more steel, and the plans govern. Lap bars to the length on the plans; if the plans are silent ask the engineer, and expect about 30 in. for a No. 4 Grade 60 bar E. Plans in this seismic category normally call for Grade 60 bar; do not substitute Grade 40 E. Dowels are tied in place before the footing inspection, never pushed into wet concrete, and bars are never heated or re-bent after the pour E.
    • Inspector checks grade, size, spacing, splices, and cover against plans V.
    • Weather and safety impalement caps on every vertical bar.
    • Crew, time, tools 2 people, 1 day; rebar cutter/bender, tie wire, chairs.
    • Done when a tape check at ten random spots matches plan spacing and cover.
    • If it goes wrong steel sitting on dirt. It must be supported, not pulled up during the pour.

    12.10Install the grounding electrode

    • What the concrete-encased electrode (often called a Ufer ground) in the footing, with a stub brought up at the future electrical service location.
    • How follow the electrical plan for location and method; leave the connection point accessible and tagged.
    • Do now for later the electrician and the state electrical inspector need this. It cannot be added after the pour.
    • Who the state electrical inspector inspects the electrode, not the county. State rule requires a concrete-encased electrode at every new building on a permanent concrete foundation V. Choose one of two routes: request a state inspection on your electrical permit while the electrode is still visible, before the pour; or attach a copper test lead to one end of the electrode, long enough to reach the service location, so the inspector can verify continuity over a span of at least 20 ft at the service inspection V. A special trip to inspect it carries a trip fee V. Buy the electrical permit (5.11) before this step and tell the inspector's office which route you are using.
    • Track note on a house built for sale this is licensed electrical work (Module 2).
    • Status V requirement and inspection routes checked against the state electrical rule (WAC 296-46B-250). Electrode material, length, and connection details come from the electrical code and are not verified here E.
    • If it goes wrong the electrode is missed or cannot be proven. The fallback is a ground ring or another electrode proven by an independent test firm at 25 ohms or less V. That costs far more than getting it seen before the pour.
    • Done when electrode is in, photographed, and the electrical inspector's requirement is met.

    12.11Pass the footing and setback inspection wait

    • What signed approval to pour footings.
    • How 1) Run the county footing checklist yourself the day before. 2) Request the inspection through the permit portal, with erosion control if not yet done. 3) Have address posted, permit and approved plans on site V.
    • Inspector checks excavation complete with forms and required reinforcement in place V; setbacks; subgrade; steel; any special inspector reports the plans require V.
    • Standard the county checklist is still labeled to the 2018 code V. Ask the inspector which edition your permit is under.
    • Done when inspection is approved in the portal. Do not schedule concrete until it is.
    • If it goes wrong a correction notice. Fix, photograph, and re-request. Never pour on a failed or missing inspection.

    12.12Pour the footings

    • What footings placed, consolidated, and struck off level.
    • How 1) Confirm truck and pump access and a washout spot. 2) Dampen dry soil. 3) Place continuously around the perimeter. 4) Consolidate around bars and dowels. 5) Strike off to form tops. 6) Leave the top rough or form a keyway where the wall will sit. 7) Re-plumb dowels before the concrete sets.
    • Weather and safety cold weather needs protection and frost-free forms, steel, and ground; hot weather needs precautions against rapid drying V. Do not pour in standing water. Wear eye protection, gloves, and boots; wet concrete burns skin.
    • Crew, time, tools 3–4 people, half a day; pump, vibrator, screed, rakes.
    • Money out concrete and pump, on delivery.
    • Done when tops are level to the forms and every dowel is plumb and in line.
    • If it goes wrong you run short. Order about 5–10% over the calculated volume E and know the supplier's short-load time before you start.

    12.13Snap wall lines and set wall forms

    • What forms for the stem walls set plumb, straight, and to height.
    • How 1) Reset strings; snap wall lines on the footing. 2) Re-check diagonals on the footing. 3) Set one side of the forms. 4) Leave the other side open for steel and embeds. 5) Coat form faces with release agent before steel goes in, never after. 6) Install ties and braces to the form supplier's layout; ask the rental yard for it in writing. 7) Shoot the top-of-wall elevation with the laser and mark it inside the forms with a chalk line or a nailed grade strip; do not assume the form tops are the pour line E.
    • Standard wall thickness per plans; plumb within 1/8 in. over a 2 ft wall; top line straight within 1/4 in. along the wall (craft) E.
    • Crew, time 2–3 people, 2–3 days with rented panels.
    • If it goes wrong the footing is slightly out of square. Correct it now by shifting the wall line within the footing, keeping at least 2 in. of projection. The dowels are already cast, so check that each one still has at least 1-1/2 in. of concrete between it and the form face after the shift. If the shift is more than about 1 in., if any dowel loses cover, or if a hold-down location moves, stop and get the engineer's written direction. Do not bend dowels that are cast in concrete to make them fit E.

    12.14Place wall steel, sleeves, and openings

    • What horizontal bars tied to the dowels, and every sleeve, vent, access opening, and beam pocket from the embed schedule boxed in.
    • How work the embed schedule line by line; fix each item to the form so the pour cannot move it; close the second form side; brace.
    • Standard crawl space vents and access per plans and code section R408; wall thickness per plans V. The state amendment requires net vent area of at least 1 sq ft per 300 sq ft of underfloor area, placed for cross ventilation; operable louvers, or less vent area than that, trigger a radon vent under the ground cover V. An access opening through the perimeter wall is at least 16 in. x 24 in. V.
    • Do now for later plumber (water and sewer sleeves), electrician (service conduit), framer (beam pockets, girder heights), HVAC (any line-set sleeve).
    • Crew, time 2 people, 1–2 days.
    • Done when every line on the embed schedule is checked off and photographed.

    12.15Mount hold-down anchors and anchor-bolt templates

    • What every hold-down anchor fixed in the forms at its exact location; every anchor bolt position marked.
    • How 1) Mark hold-down locations from a building corner, not from the previous anchor. 2) Hang each from a template across the form top. 3) Set embedment and projection to the hardware maker's table. 4) Tie it off the rebar. 5) Mark anchor bolt positions on the form, or better, hang the bolts now from wood or manufactured bolt holders across the form top. Wet-set bolts lean, sink, and get voids around them; pre-hung bolts are inspected in place E. 6) Before buying bolts, read the shear wall schedule for bolt diameter and sill plate thickness: some shear walls call for a 3x sill, which needs a longer bolt to keep 7 in. of embedment. Set projection to the plate thickness plus about 1 in. for washer and nut: about 2-1/2 in. for a 2x sill and 3-1/2 in. for a 3x E.
    • Standard the code floor for anchor bolts is 1/2 in. diameter, no more than 6 ft apart, at least 7 in. into concrete, in the middle third of the plate width, at least two per plate piece with one no more than 12 in. and no less than 7 bolt diameters from each end V. Engineered shear walls in this seismic category usually call for 5/8 in. bolts at closer spacing that changes from wall to wall, so lay out each wall from the shear wall schedule, never from the 6 ft rule E. In Seismic Design Category D2 a plate washer at least 3 in. x 3 in. x 0.229 in. goes on every anchor bolt along a braced or shear wall line V. Check the plans for 3x sill plates and for bolts offset toward the sheathed face, and set bolt projection and position to suit E. Location within about 1/4 in. (craft) E. Hold-down anchors follow the maker's sheet. For the common cast-in hold-down bolt that means in place before the pour, at least 1-3/4 in. from the concrete edge, a No. 4 bar 3 to 5 in. below the top of the wall, concrete of at least 2,500 psi, and a wall at least 6 in. thick, or 8 in. for the 7/8 in. bolt V. Never cut, bend, or weld a hold-down anchor or swap one model for another without the engineer's written approval E.
    • Inspector checks anchor bolts may be set in wet concrete; hold-down straps and their embedded bolts may not. Hold-downs per stamped plans and maker's instructions V.
    • Do now for later bolts must miss door openings, plate splices, and stud locations where posts or hold-downs land. Lay out against the wall framing plan.
    • Crew, time 1–2 people, half a day.
    • Done when the hold-down schedule is fully checked off with photos showing a tape.
    • If it goes wrong a misplaced hold-down found after the pour needs the engineer's written fix. Anchors installed into cured concrete (epoxy or wedge) require special inspection unless the engineer waives it and the county approves V. The engineer picks the product, diameter, embedment, and edge distance; a retrofit anchor near the edge or end of a wall often holds far less than the cast-in bolt it replaces E. Adhesive anchors need concrete at least 21 days old V, a hole drilled, brushed, and blown clean exactly as the maker's instructions say, and the special inspector on site while you install, not afterward E.

    12.16Pass the foundation wall inspection wait

    • What signed approval to pour walls.
    • How self-check against the county checklist, request inspection, keep one form face open or accessible so steel and embeds are visible.
    • Inspector checks wall thickness, steel, cover, hold-downs, vents, sleeves, height above grade V.
    • Done when approved in the portal.

    12.17Pour the walls and set anchor bolts

    • What walls placed in lifts, consolidated, top struck level, anchor bolts set.
    • How 1) Place in lifts around the perimeter, not all in one spot. 2) Vibrate each lift, briefly, and around every embed. 3) Watch forms for movement; stop if anything shifts. 4) Strike the top to the level line. 5) Set anchor bolts at the marks right after strike-off, while the concrete is still plastic, and vibrate or rod around each one; never force a bolt into concrete that has started to stiffen. Bolts hung from templates before the pour are more accurate and are the better practice E. Hold-down anchors are never set this way (12.15). 6) Re-check hold-downs for plumb.
    • Standard top of wall level within 1/4 in. across the building (craft) E. Where bolts resist placement, vibrate so concrete fully surrounds them V.
    • Weather and safety same limits as 12.12. Keep people clear of the pump boom and away from form faces during placement.
    • Crew, time, tools 4 people, half a day; pump, vibrator, laser, floats.
    • Money out concrete and pump.
    • Done when top is level, bolts are plumb with correct projection, forms held.
    • If it goes wrong a form bulges or blows out. Stop the pump, brace or shovel out, re-form. Have spare braces, stakes, and plywood staged before the truck arrives.

    12.18Cure and protect wait

    • What concrete kept moist and protected until it gains strength.
    • How leave forms on at least a day E; cover or mist in dry weather; insulate in cold.
    • Standard follow the plans or the supplier's guidance for curing time. Where they are silent, keep the concrete moist and above about 50°F for 7 days and never let it freeze in the first days E. Until at least 7 days have passed, longer in cold weather, do not tighten anchor bolt or hold-down nuts, stack lumber on the walls, or backfill E.
    • Done when the curing period on your plan or from your supplier has passed.

    12.19Strip forms and measure the as-built foundation

    • What an as-built drawing with actual lengths, diagonals, top-of-wall elevations, and bolt locations.
    • How 1) Strip and clean forms; return rentals. 2) Measure every wall and both diagonals. 3) Shoot the top of wall at each corner and every 8–10 ft. 4) Check each hold-down against the plan.
    • Standard if the foundation is out by more than about 1/2 in. in square or level E, decide the correction with the framer before lumber is cut.
    • Do now for later give the framer the as-built. They correct small errors at the mudsill.
    • Inspector checks honeycombing or exposed steel may draw a correction.
    • Done when as-built is in the binder and shared with the framer.

    12.20Install dampproofing and foundation drainage where required wait

    • What exterior coating and footing drain as the plans and site require.
    • How apply coating to the exterior below grade; lay perforated pipe in washed rock with filter fabric, sloped to daylight or an approved discharge; keep roof drain lines separate.
    • Standard below-grade habitable space requires waterproofing or dampproofing, checked at the drainage inspection V. Crawl space requirements depend on soil and site; follow the plans. Where groundwater can rise near the floor or surface water does not drain away, the model code requires the crawl space floor to be as high as outside grade unless an approved drainage system is provided V. Most crawl spaces here are dug below outside grade, so plan on a footing drain and a crawl space low-point drain to daylight unless the plans and the inspector say neither is needed E. A crawl space that holds winter water is the most common foundation complaint in this region and is very costly to fix after backfill.
    • Inspector checks the county's Drainage/Waterproofing inspection, before backfill: footing and downspout drains located and routed to their connection or termination point, drain rock around the footing drain pipe with filter fabric on top, and waterproofing where interior space is below grade V.
    • Done when inspection approved and photos taken before cover.
    • If it goes wrong backfilled before inspection. You dig it up.

    12.21Manage crawl space water

    • What a way for water to leave the crawl space, and every pipe that must run beside the foundation laid before backfill.
    • How 1) Find the low point of the crawl space floor. 2) Give it an outlet: a drain through or under the footing to daylight where the lot allows, or a sump basin for a pump where it does not. 3) Lay the downspout tightlines (solid pipe) along the foundation to their discharge point, separate from the footing drain. 4) Confirm the footing drain outfall is open and protected. 5) Photograph everything with a tape before cover.
    • Options gravity drain to daylight (no moving parts; needs fall); sump and pump (works on flat lots; needs power and upkeep); neither, where the plans and soil show a dry site.
    • Recommendation gravity wherever the lot gives you fall. Set the basin now even if you never fit a pump; it cannot be added cheaply later E.
    • Standard discharge goes where the approved drainage plan says, not to the septic field or a neighbor E · confirm against your permit conditions. Roof water and footing drains stay in separate pipes until the discharge point (12.20).
    • Why now final grading and downspout connections happen in 18.2, but trenching against a finished, backfilled foundation then is slow and risks the drain and dampproofing.
    • Weather the crawl space is an open pond until the roof is on. Keep the ground cover out until dry-in (13.19) and pump standing water rather than leaving it against the footings.
    • Done when water poured at the high end of the crawl space reaches the outlet, and every tightline is in, capped above grade, and photographed.

    12.22Prepare the crawl space

    • What pier pads complete, debris and form lumber removed, ground graded to drain, vapor barrier staged.
    • Standard wood closer to ground than code clearances (joists 18 in., beams 12 in.) must be treated or decay-resistant V.
    • Do now for later remove all wood scraps; they attract insects. Confirm crawl access is usable for the plumber and inspector.
    • Done when crawl space is clean and drains to its low point.

    12.23Backfill and rough grade

    • What soil placed against the walls in lifts and graded to drain away.
    • How backfill both sides evenly in lifts; keep heavy equipment back from the wall; slope finished rough grade away.
    • Standard fall of 6 in. in the first 10 ft V. Walls retaining more than 48 in. of unbalanced fill without top support need engineered design V.
    • Weather and safety a wall holding back 4 ft or more of fill on one side is not backfilled until the floor framing is anchored to it or it is braced, and the concrete has gained strength E. Place fill in lifts of about 8 to 12 in., use only hand-operated compactors within about 3 ft of the wall, and keep excavators and loaded trucks back from the wall by at least the height of the fill E. Keep frozen lumps, wood, and rocks over about 6 in. out of the fill against the wall E.
    • Done when grade slopes away on all sides and no wall shows movement or cracking.

    12.24Pour the garage slab, if the house has an attached garage

    • What the garage floor slab. The assumed build has no attached garage; skip this step if yours has none.
    • How 1) Compact the base in lifts and place the rock base the plans call for. 2) Install any under-slab piping or conduit and have it inspected. 3) Place the vapor barrier and reinforcement where the plans show them. 4) Set isolation joint material against the stem walls and any posts. 5) Set screeds so the slab slopes to the vehicle door. 6) Pour, finish, cut control joints, and cure.
    • Options pour now (truck and pump access is easy; slab is a clean work surface for framing); pour after dry-in (protected from rain and from framing damage; harder access). Hire a flatwork crew, or place and finish it yourself.
    • Recommendation hire the finishing. A slab is finished in one short window and a poor finish is permanent E.
    • Standard thickness, reinforcement, and vapor barrier per the plans. The model code requires garage floors to slope toward the vehicle door or a drain E · confirm against your plans.
    • Inspector checks under-slab plumbing or mechanical before cover, and an under-slab inspection where the slab has in-slab piping, insulation, or a vapor barrier E · ask the county which apply to your permit.
    • Track T2 contractor rules in Module 2 apply to self-performed work.
    • Done when slab is poured, jointed, curing, and protected from traffic for the period the supplier gives.
    • If it goes wrong slab poured over uncompacted fill beside the stem wall settles and cracks. Compact in thin lifts or use rock.

    12.25Close out the module

    • What binder updated, bills paid, schedule and budget reset.
    • How 1) File inspection approvals, as-built, embed photos, concrete delivery tickets. 2) Collect lien waivers from any hired trade and supplier. 3) Update cost-to-complete. 4) Request the lender draw if one is tied to foundation. 5) Confirm lumber and framing dates.
    • Done when the module gate below is fully checked.

    Assumed build: single story, crawl space, dimensional or I-joist floor on a mudsill, 2x6 exterior walls, engineered trusses, composition shingles.

    Outcome: when you finish this module you will have a framed, braced, inspected structure with a finished roof: weather-tight from above and ready for windows and rough-in.

    Before you start: foundation as-built (12.19), structural sheets, shear wall and hold-down schedule, truss drawings, lumber and hardware on order (9.6), fall protection (8.6).

    Inspections: underfloor (13.6); sheathing and shear (13.18). The framing inspection comes after rough-in (15.16).

    Points of no return: subfloor over an uninspected floor system (13.7); roof sheathing over mis-set trusses (13.17).

    Never change in the field without the engineer: trusses, engineered beams and I-joists, hold-downs and their anchors, shear wall nailing and sheathing, post and beam sizes, anchor bolts, and any opening added or enlarged in a shear wall.

    Variant — prescriptive wall bracing: this module assumes engineered shear walls. If your plans use the code's braced wall panels instead, 13.9 and 13.12 change: panel locations and lengths come from the bracing plan, fastening from the code table, and anchorage from the bracing method. That variant is not written in this draft; have the designer mark every braced panel on the plans.

    Track note: on T2, self-performed framing on a house built for sale falls under the contractor rules in Module 2.

    13.1Review framing plans and build lists

    • What a cut list, a hardware list by location, and a marked-up plan showing every shear wall, hold-down, beam, and point load.
    • How highlight shear walls by type on the floor plan; write the nailing for each beside it; trace every point load from roof to foundation.
    • Standard point loads continue to the foundation V.
    • Done when you can state, for any wall, its type, nailing, and hardware without opening the engineering.

    13.2Order lumber and confirm trusses

    • What the lumber package scheduled in drops (floor, walls, roof), and a confirmed truss delivery date and truck access.
    • Standard sheathing grade and thickness match the plans V. Order pressure-treated plates and the correct fasteners for them.
    • If it goes wrong everything arrives at once and sits in the rain. Stage deliveries.

    13.3Install the mudsill

    • What pressure-treated sill plates bolted to the foundation, straight and square, over a sill gasket.
    • How 1) Snap lines square to the as-built, correcting small foundation errors. 2) Lay out and drill for anchor bolts with a bit no more than 1/16 in. larger than the bolt; an oversized or slotted hole lets the wall slide before the bolt takes load E. 3) Place sill seal. 4) Set plates, washers, and nuts. 5) Shim low spots with full-width, non-compressible shims at every bolt and under every post and stud pack, and grout gaps over about 1/4 in.; check level E. 6) Snug the nuts, then check them again just before cover because the plate shrinks as it dries E. Never notch the plate, slot a hole, bend a bolt, or leave one out to make the plate fit.
    • Standard plates on concrete are treated or naturally decay-resistant wood V. At least two bolts per plate piece, no more than 6 ft apart, within 12 in. of each end and at least 7 bolt diameters from the end, in the middle third of the plate V. Plate washers at least 3 in. x 3 in. x 0.229 in., hot-dipped galvanized on treated plates; slotted washers need a standard cut washer under the nut V. Level within 1/8 in. over 10 ft (craft) E.
    • Inspector checks bolt size and spacing against the shear schedule; washers; nuts tightened V.
    • Crew, time 2 people, 1 day.
    • If it goes wrong a bolt lands at a plate end or is missing. Do not improvise; an added anchor in cured concrete may need the engineer and a special inspection (12.15).

    13.4Set posts, beams, and girders

    • What interior supports installed on pier pads with positive connections.
    • Standard posts connected positively to pads and beams; girder joints over supports; beams at least 12 in. and joists at least 18 in. above grade unless treated V. Posts on concrete need a barrier or pedestal per plan V.
    • How set beams to a string line at mudsill height; crown up. Build every beam, post, and connector exactly as sized on the plans: a different species or grade, a smaller or different engineered beam, or a different connector needs the engineer's written approval first E. Glulam, LVL, and PSL beams are not notched or drilled except as the maker's guide allows, and a glulam goes in with its stamped top up E. Where a beam sits in a concrete pocket, bear on at least 3 in. and leave 1/2 in. of air space at the sides and end, or use treated wood E. Under every point load marked on the plans, carry a full-width post or squash blocks through the floor framing to the pier or footing sized for it E.

    13.5Frame the floor

    • What joists, rim, blocking, and hangers installed per the layout.
    • How mark layout on the plates; crown joists up; install hangers with the specified nails; block at bearing points.
    • Standard joist bearing at least 1-1/2 in. on wood or steel and 3 in. on concrete; joists lapped at least 3 in. over supports; solid blocking at ends and bearing points; I-joists installed to the manufacturer's guide, which must be on site V. Never cut or notch an I-joist flange; holes go only in the web and only where the maker's hole chart allows E. Sawn joists: notches no deeper than one-sixth of the joist depth and none in the middle third of the span, end notches no deeper than one-quarter of the depth, holes no larger than one-third of the depth and at least 2 in. from the top and bottom edges V. Trusses, glulam, LVL, and I-joists are not cut, notched, or drilled at all unless the design or the maker allows that exact hole V.
    • Later trades check toilet, tub, and floor register locations against the joist layout now; a flange centered on a joist is a classic conflict V. Leave plumbing chases clear.
    • Crew, time 2–3 people, 3–5 days E.

    13.6Pass underfloor inspection wait

    • What approval of the floor frame, and of any plumbing, ducts, or wiring under the floor, before the subfloor covers it.
    • How complete under-floor plumbing and duct runs that will be hard to reach later; have them inspected with or before the framing check.
    • Inspector checks clearances to grade; hardware and anchor bolts; hangers; blocking; foundation undamaged by backfill; crawl vents at 1 sq ft per 300 sq ft of underfloor area V.
    • If it goes wrong you sheath first. The inspector can require openings cut in the new subfloor.

    13.7Sheath the subfloor

    • What subfloor panels glued and fastened.
    • How run panels perpendicular to joists; stagger end joints; apply subfloor adhesive to each joist just ahead of the panel; fasten per the schedule; leave the manufacturer's gap between panels.
    • Standard fastener type and spacing per plans; a 1/8 in. gap at panel edges unless the panel is self-spacing E.
    • Weather a subfloor exposed for weeks in rain swells at the edges. Use panels rated for exposure, sweep standing water, and drill drain holes if water ponds E.

    13.8Lay out walls

    • What every wall, opening, and stud location marked on the subfloor and plates.
    • How 1) Snap lines for all walls. 2) Check diagonals of the whole floor. 3) Cut top and bottom plates in pairs and mark layout on both together. 4) Mark windows, doors, posts, hold-down studs, and intersecting walls.
    • Standard lines square within 1/8 in.; layout pulled from the same end on every wall so sheathing joints land on studs E.
    • Later trades mark tub and shower valve locations and cabinet runs so studs do not land in the way.

    13.9Frame and raise exterior walls

    • What exterior walls built flat, sheathed where practical, and stood up.
    • How build headers and trimmers first; nail the wall together on the deck; square it by diagonals; sheath and nail per the shear schedule while it is flat; raise with enough people or wall jacks; brace immediately. Before sheathing flat, read the shear wall details: where they show sheathing lapping down over the rim joist or sill, or a blocked horizontal joint at a set height, let the panels hang below the bottom plate by that amount or sheath that wall after it is standing. Panels cut flush with the bottom plate cannot be fixed later E. Leave a 1/8 in. gap between panels V. Build in lengths the crew can lift: a sheathed 2x6 wall weighs roughly 30 to 40 lb per foot of length, so four people should stop at about 20 ft or use wall jacks E.
    • Standard double or triple trimmers under headers per the tables and plans; stud size, grade, and spacing per plans V.
    • Safety a 2x6 sheathed wall is heavy. Plan each lift, clear the fall zone, and nail stop blocks at the deck edge so the plate cannot kick out.
    • Crew, time 3–4 people on lift days.

    13.10Frame interior walls

    • What interior partitions and bearing walls stood and tied in.
    • Standard bearing walls and posts line up with the supports below.

    13.11Plumb, line, and brace

    • What walls plumb at corners, straight along the top, and held by temporary braces.
    • How plumb corners with a long level or laser; string the top plates and push or pull to the line; install the double top plate with laps at corners and intersections.
    • Standard plumb within 1/4 in. in 8 ft; top plate straight within 1/4 in. along its length (craft) E. End joints in the two top plates are offset at least 24 in. as a code floor. Engineered plans in this seismic category usually call for a 48 in. lap with a set number of nails, because the top plates carry earthquake force along the wall line; follow the plan detail E. The 16-gauge by 1-1/2 in. metal tie is a different rule: it is required where a top plate is cut or bored more than half its width for pipe or duct, and it does not replace the splice offset V.

    13.12Install hold-downs, straps, and shear nailing

    • What all lateral hardware and shear nailing complete.
    • How work the shear schedule wall by wall and check off each.
    • Standard common nails of the size on the shear schedule, not box or sinker nails; panel edges blocked or landing on framing; full-height studs at hold-downs and straps, with multiple studs where specified; nails in every hold-down stud; hardware installed to the plans and maker's instructions V. Drive nails flush, at least 3/8 in. from the panel edge E. Up to 20% of the nails around a panel's perimeter may be overdriven by no more than 1/8 in.; past that, add one nail for every two overdriven, or get the engineer's fix V. Where the schedule calls for 3x studs or plates, or doubled studs, at closely nailed panel joints, they are required and the nails are staggered E. Sheathing edge nailing goes into the hold-down post itself, and each hold-down gets the exact screws, bolts, or nails the maker lists, in every hole E. No substitutions: a different panel thickness or grade, nail, stud size, hold-down, or strap changes the wall's capacity and needs the engineer's written approval. Do not notch or bore a hold-down post E.
    • Inspector checks this list, on the sheathing and shear inspection, before anything covers it V.
    • If it goes wrong the nail gun is set too deep and breaks the panel face. Stop, adjust, and ask the engineer how to correct the overdriven area.

    13.13Set trusses

    • What trusses set at layout, plumb, and temporarily braced.
    • How 1) Mark layout on the top plates. 2) Stage trusses flat and supported. 3) Lift by crane or boom truck. 4) Set and brace the first truss rigidly to the ground or deck. 5) Set each next truss with spacers and lateral braces as you go.
    • Standard truss drawings on site, stamped; configuration, bearing, and hangers per the drawings; truss members are never cut, notched, drilled, spliced, or altered without written approval from a registered design professional, and loads the truss was not designed for (air handler, water heater, storage platform, solar) are not added without the same check V. A truss that arrives or becomes cracked, has a loose or missing plate, or is broken in handling is set aside; get a sealed repair detail from the truss supplier before it is installed or covered E.
    • Safety unbraced trusses fall like dominoes. Follow the truss industry's bracing guide (BCSI) that ships with the package. Its core rules: ground bracing is built before the first truss is lifted and the first truss is fastened to it; rows of top chord lateral restraint are no more than 10 ft apart for spans up to 30 ft and 8 ft apart for spans of 30 to 45 ft; restraints are 2x4 or larger and lap over at least two trusses; diagonal braces go on the top and bottom chord planes at least every 10 truss spaces (20 ft); bottom chord restraint rows are 10 to 15 ft apart V. Spacer blocks alone are not bracing: without diagonals the whole set racks over together E. Do not stack plywood or OSB more than 16 in. high on trusses, and never at midspan or the peak V. Do not set trusses in gusty wind, and do not release the crane until the truss is restrained E. No one stands under a lifted truss.
    • Crew, time, tools 3–4 people plus crane operator; 1 day for a simple roof.
    • Money out crane, by the hour.

    13.14Install permanent truss bracing

    • What all permanent bracing, blocking, and tie-downs shown on the truss drawings and plans.
    • Standard bracing complete per the truss engineer's plans; ganged trusses fastened per the manufacturer; roof-to-wall connectors per plans V.
    • Load path this is where roof forces reach the walls: blocking between truss heels with the nailing or clips on the plans, an uplift connector at every truss, gable end bracing, and any drag straps. It is the item most often missing at the sheathing inspection E.
    • Later trades frame the attic access now: at least 22 in. x 30 in. with 30 in. of headroom above V.

    13.15Set up scaffold and staging

    • What a safe working platform at the eaves and gables for roof edge framing, sheathing, roofing, and later the siding.
    • How 1) Choose the system. 2) Set it on firm, level bearing. 3) Tie it to the building as the maker requires. 4) Fit guardrails and access. 5) Inspect it before each shift and after wind.
    • Options pump jacks with aluminum poles (cheap to own, good for long straight walls, two people to set); rented frame scaffold (stable, slow to move); towable boom lift (fast on rough ground, one or two workers only, weekly rent); wall brackets and planks (framers' method; needs experience).
    • Recommendation rented frame scaffold or pump jacks on the eave walls, plus a boom lift for the week of gable and rake work E.
    • Standard Washington's fall protection rules apply from 4 ft on most surfaces (8.6). Guardrails, a personal fall arrest system, or both, by the system and task.
    • Money out rent it through siding (Module 14), not just through roofing. Returning and re-renting costs more than holding it E.
    • Done when every roof edge can be reached from a guarded platform or a tied-off position.

    13.16Frame eaves and rakes

    • What truss tails trimmed, sub-fascia and fascia on, rake (gable overhang) framing built, and eave blocking installed.
    • How 1) Snap a line across the truss tails and cut them to it. 2) Install the sub-fascia, straightened to a string. 3) Build the rake overhangs as the truss drawings and plans show (outlookers or a dropped gable truss). 4) Install the eave blocking between trusses shown on the plans, using vented blocks where the attic vents through them. 5) Install and prime the fascia and rake boards.
    • Standard blocking and its nailing per the plans. The roof edge nailing into that blocking is part of the lateral design and is slow to add from below later E.
    • Why now drip edge laps over the fascia (13.19), and roof sheathing runs out over the rake framing (13.17). Neither can wait for exterior trim (14.9).
    • Crew, time 2 people, 1–2 days on a simple gable roof E.
    • Done when fascia is straight to a string, rakes are built, and every blocking bay on the plans is filled.

    13.17Sheath the roof

    • What roof panels installed and nailed.
    • How eave and rake framing complete first (13.16). Start square to the eave; stagger joints; leave a 1/8 in. gap at panel ends and edges unless the panel maker says otherwise V; use panel clips where specified; nail per the schedule.
    • Standard fasteners per plan. Leave the ridge or other vent openings the roof design calls for.
    • Safety fall protection from the first sheet.

    13.18Pass sheathing and shear inspection wait

    • What approval of sheathing, shear nailing, and hardware.
    • Standard this inspection occurs before any water-resistive barrier, cladding, window or door flashing, or insulation goes on V.
    • How leave all wall sheathing and hardware exposed; have plans and truss drawings on site. When you request it, ask whether the inspector also wants to see roof sheathing nailing before underlayment covers it: the county checklist covers diaphragm nailing but does not mention roofing underlayment V. If so, request the inspection for the day roof sheathing finishes and ask whether underlayment may go on first, with photos, when rain is forecast E.
    • If it goes wrong wrap goes on first to beat the rain. You will be removing it.

    13.19Dry in the roof

    • What drip edge and underlayment installed over the whole roof.
    • How fascia and rake (barge) boards must already be on, straight, and primed, because drip edge laps over them (13.16); they cannot wait for 14.9. Then eave drip edge, then underlayment lapped shingle-fashion from the eave up, then rake drip edge over the underlayment E. Use synthetic underlayment rated for the weeks of exposure you expect, fastened with cap fasteners; felt wrinkles and tears when it sits through rain E.
    • Standard follow the roofing manufacturer's instructions; they are what the warranty and the inspector rely on.
    • Weather this is the dry-in milestone. Schedule it the same day or the day after sheathing.

    13.20Install roofing and flashings

    • What shingles, valley and wall flashings, pipe boots, and vents.
    • Options DIY (large savings; real fall risk); hire a roofer (fast, warranted, often one to two days).
    • Recommendation hire it unless the roof is low-slope and simple and you have fall arrest gear and experience.
    • Standard step flashing at every wall, kick-out flashing where a roof edge meets a wall, and plumbing vents extending at least 6 in. above the roof V · for vent height; E otherwise.
    • Later trades all roof penetrations (plumbing vents, exhaust vents) should be located before roofing. Coordinate with 15.1.

    13.21Install blocking and backing for later trades

    • What every piece of backing the finishes will need.
    • List E cabinets (upper and lower rails); bath grab bars and towel bars; shower doors; tub and shower edges; handrails; wall-hung televisions and shelves; closet rods; curtain rods; stair and guard posts; drywall backing at corners and ceilings; fire blocking at soffits and dropped ceilings; crawl access framed at least 18 in. x 24 in. V.
    • How walk each room with the cabinet and fixture plans and a can of marking paint.
    • Standard photograph every wall with a tape in frame before cover (15.13).

    13.22Close out

    • What dry-in checklist complete, lumber returns made, schedule and cost report updated, draw requested.
    • Standard framing lumber should be drying. Wet framing delays insulation and drywall; the framing inspection looks for significant moisture V. Buy a pin moisture meter and log readings at bottom plates, sills, and north walls. A working target is 19% or less before insulation, and lower before drywall and wood flooring E. After a wet-season frame, budget two to four weeks of fans, dehumidifiers, and heat once the house is closed in E.

    Design brief

    The transformation

    • T1 from "we could probably build a house" to living in a permitted, inspected house you built, with records that prove how.

    Course outcomes

    By the end, the learner can:

    1. Decide go or no-go from numbers, and pick a track knowing its legal limits.
    2. Buy a lot only after proving it is buildable at a known cost.
    3. Produce permit-ready plans that fit the lot, the budget, and their own skills.
    4. Hold a line-item budget and schedule and keep both current weekly.
    5. Hire, contract, and pay trades without disputes or liens.
    6. Build each phase to the standard an inspector and a good framer expect, and pass each inspection.
    7. Obtain occupancy and either move in or sell with complete records.
    8. Review the project and decide whether to do it again.

    • Knows tools, equipment operation, basic carpentry and concrete, reading a tape and a level.
